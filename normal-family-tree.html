The Normal Family Tree

Every distribution on this page is built from independent standard Normals with three moves: square, add and divide. This sheet shows how the family fits together, then gives each member's density, MGF, kth moment, mean and variance, with each key derivation cut down to its main idea.

How each member is made

Read each step as a recipe: what you start with, what you do to it, and what comes out. The step numbers match the arrows above. One running example with real numbers goes through all the steps, so you can watch each new distribution being built out of the previous ones.

Building from Z · steps 1–8

  1. 1

    Standard Normal

    made from any Normal

    used in steps 2, 5
    Start with
    A Normal variable X with mean μ and standard deviation σ.
    Do this
    subtract μ→divide by σ

    Subtracting μ moves the centre to 0. Dividing by σ makes the spread 1.

    You get
    \(Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)\)
    With numbers
    Heights X ~ N(170, 10²). A height of 185 gives (185 − 170) / 10 = 1.5. So Z = 1.5 means "1.5 standard deviations above the mean".
    Why this shape
    Shifting and rescaling move and stretch the bell without bending it, so the result is still a bell, now centred at 0 with spread 1.

    Proved byMGF scaling: \(M_Z(t)=e^{-\mu t/\sigma}M_X(t/\sigma)=e^{t^2/2}\).

  2. 2

    Chi-square, 1 df

    made from one Standard Normal

    used in steps 3, 7
    Start with
    One standard Normal Z.
    Do this
    square it
    You get
    \(Y=Z^2\sim\chi^2(1)\)
    With numbers
    Z = 1.5 gives 2.25. Z = −1.5 also gives 2.25, because the sign disappears. Z = 0.3 gives 0.09, so small values get even smaller.
    Why this shape
    Every value becomes positive, and the many Z's near 0 get squashed even closer to 0. The curve is a spike at 0 with a long right tail. Its mean is \(E(Z^2)=1\).

    Proved byCDF method: \(P(Z^2\le y)=P(-\sqrt y\le Z\le \sqrt y)=2\Phi(\sqrt y)-1\), then differentiate.

  3. 3

    Chi-square, n df

    made from n independent Chi-square(1)'s

    used in steps 4, 5, 6
    Start with
    n independent standard Normals \(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\).
    Do this
    square each one (step 2)→add the squares
    You get
    \(Y=Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\cdots+Z_n^2\sim\chi^2(n)\)
    With numbers
    n = 3, with draws 1.5, −0.4, 0.8. The squares are 2.25, 0.16 and 0.64, so Y = 3.05. That is one value from a χ²(3).
    Why this shape
    Each square adds 1 to the mean and 2 to the variance, so Y has mean n and variance 2n, and the hump moves right as n grows. "Degrees of freedom" is the number of squares you added.

    Proved byMGFs multiply: \(\big[(1-2t)^{-1/2}\big]^n=(1-2t)^{-n/2}\).

  4. 4

    Gamma distribution

    the same thing as Chi-square(n), under another name

    Start with
    The χ²(n) density.
    Do this
    put α = n/2 and θ = 2 into the Gamma density

    Nothing is done to the variable. You only compare the two formulas.

    You get
    \(\chi^2(n)=\text{Gamma}\big(\alpha=\tfrac n2,\ \theta=2\big)\)
    With numbers
    χ²(3) is Gamma(1.5, 2). Gamma mean αθ = 1.5 × 2 = 3 = n. Gamma variance αθ² = 1.5 × 4 = 6 = 2n.
    Why it matters
    Every Gamma formula, such as the MGF and the moments, works for χ² straight away.

    Proved bycomparing the two density formulas.

  5. 5

    Student's t, n df

    made from a Standard Normal and an independent Chi-square(n)

    used in steps 7, 8 and Student's theorem
    Start with
    Two independent things: a new standard Normal Z, and a χ²(n) value Y from step 3.
    Do this
    divide Y by n→take the square root→divide Z by it

    Y/n is an average of n squared Z's, so it is usually close to 1.

    You get
    \(T=\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{Y/n}}\sim t(n)\)
    With numbers
    Z = 1.2 and Y = 3.05 from step 3 (n = 3): Y/n = 1.017, √1.017 = 1.008, T = 1.2 / 1.008 = 1.19, almost the same as Z.
    If Y had come out small, say 0.3: Y/n = 0.1, √0.1 = 0.316, T = 1.2 / 0.316 = 3.79, far out in the tail.
    Why this shape
    Usually the denominator is near 1 and T ≈ Z. Now and then it is small and T is thrown far out. So t is a bell like Z, but with heavier tails.

    Proved bythe Jacobian method: joint density of (Z, Y), change variables to (T, Y), then integrate Y out.

  6. 6

    F distribution

    made from two independent Chi-squares

    used in step 7
    Start with
    Two independent χ² values: U ~ χ²(n₁) and V ~ χ²(n₂).
    Do this
    divide each by its own df→divide the first by the second

    After dividing by its df, each one is an average close to 1.

    You get
    \(F=\dfrac{U/n_1}{V/n_2}\sim F(n_1,n_2)\)
    With numbers
    U = 3.05 with n₁ = 3 gives 1.017. V = 4.6 with n₂ = 5 gives 0.92. F = 1.017 / 0.92 = 1.11, close to 1.
    Why this shape
    A ratio of two numbers near 1 sits near 1, is never negative and has a right tail. Dividing two sample variances gives exactly this ratio, which is why F is used to compare variances.

    Proved bythe Jacobian method, the same recipe as step 5.

  7. 7

    F(1, n)

    made from Student's t

    Start with
    A t(n) value, \(T=Z/\sqrt{Y/n}\) from step 5.
    Do this
    square it

    The top becomes Z², which is a χ²(1) by step 2. The bottom becomes Y/n.

    You get
    \(T^2=\dfrac{Z^2/1}{Y/n}\sim F(1,n)\)
    With numbers
    T = 1.19 gives T² = 1.42. The same value comes from Z² / (Y/n) = 1.44 / 1.017 = 1.42.
    Why
    The top is a χ² divided by its df (1) and the bottom is a χ² divided by its df (n). That is the recipe of step 6 with n₁ = 1 and n₂ = n.

    Proved bythe definitions alone.

  8. 8

    Back to the Standard Normal

    what t(n) becomes when n is large

    Start with
    \(T=Z/\sqrt{Y/n}\) from step 5.
    Do this
    let n grow

    Y/n is an average of n squared Z's, with mean 1 and standard deviation √(2/n), which shrinks as n grows.

    You get
    \(t(n)\to N(0,1)\ \text{ as } n\to\infty\)
    With numbers
    The spread of the denominator Y/n is 0.82 for n = 3, 0.26 for n = 30 and 0.08 for n = 300. It is pinned at 1, so T ≈ Z.
    Why it matters
    This is why t tables approach z tables. The 2.5% point is 2.04 for t(30) and 1.96 for Z.

    Proved by\(\big(1+\frac{t^2}{n}\big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\to e^{-t^2/2}\).

The glue and the tools

Two special functions supply every normalizing constant on this page. Three methods produce every new density.

Gamma function

  • \(\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{\alpha-1}\,dx,\ \ \alpha \gt 0\)
  • \(\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)\,\Gamma(\alpha-1),\ \ \Gamma(n)=(n-1)!\)
    Integration by parts; the boundary term dies because exponentials beat powers.
  • \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi,\ \ \Gamma(\tfrac32)=\tfrac12\sqrt\pi\)
    Put \(x=u^2/2\): it becomes \(\sqrt2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}du = \sqrt2\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt{2\pi}\).
  • Workhorse: \(\int_0^\infty x^{a-1}e^{-x/\theta}\,dx=\theta^{a}\,\Gamma(a)\)
    Most derivations below end with this integral.

Beta function

  • \(B(\alpha,\beta)=\int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,dx\)
  • \(B(\alpha,\beta)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}\)
    Multiply two Gamma integrals, substitute \(x=uv,\ y=u(1-v)\), \(|J|=u\).
  • \(B(\alpha,\beta)=B(\beta,\alpha),\ \ B(1,1)=1,\ \ B(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)=\pi\)
    Symmetry from \(x \to 1-x\).

Why they show up everywhere. A density needs a constant that makes its area 1. A χ² density is one Gamma-type integral, so \(\Gamma(\frac n2)\) appears. t and F are built from two independent pieces. When the helper variable is integrated out, the two Gammas merge into \(\Gamma(a+b)\) on top of \(\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\), and that ratio is \(1/B(a,b)\).

The parent

Normal

\(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\quad Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)\)

Every other member is made from independent copies of Z. Standardizing changes the ruler and leaves the shape alone.

Density
\(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2},\quad -\infty \lt x \lt \infty\)
MGF
\(M_X(t)=e^{\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2}\) for all t, and \(M_Z(t)=e^{t^2/2}\)
kth moment
\(E(Z^k)=0\) for odd k, and \(E(Z^{2m})=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2m-1)=\dfrac{(2m)!}{2^m\,m!}\)So \(E(Z^2)=1,\ E(Z^4)=3\). Central moments of X: \(E[(X-\mu)^k]=\sigma^kE(Z^k)\).
Mean
\(\mu\)
Variance
\(\sigma^2\)

Key derivations standard results

  1. \(I^2=\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\)

    Square \(I=\int e^{-z^2/2}dz\) and switch to polar coordinates. So \(I=\sqrt{2\pi}\).

  2. \(tz-\tfrac{z^2}{2}=-\tfrac{(z-t)^2}{2}+\tfrac{t^2}{2}\)

    Complete the square in the MGF integral. What remains is an N(t, 1) density with area 1, so \(M_Z(t)=e^{t^2/2}\).

  3. \(M_{\mu+\sigma Z}(t)=e^{\mu t}M_Z(\sigma t)\)

    Scaling rule \(M_{aX+b}(t)=e^{bt}M_X(at)\) gives the general MGF.

LinksThe same Gaussian integral gives \(\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt\pi\). Square Z to get χ²(1). The even moments are the χ²(1) moments, because \(Z^{2m}=(Z^2)^m\).

Square it

Chi-square with 1 degree of freedom

\(Y=Z^2\sim\chi^2(1)\)

Squaring pushes values near 0 even closer to 0, so the mass piles up against zero. The density is infinite at 0 and has a long right tail.

Density
\(g(y)=\dfrac{y^{-1/2}\,e^{-y/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=\dfrac{y^{\frac12-1}e^{-y/2}}{2^{1/2}\,\Gamma(\frac12)},\quad y \gt 0\)
MGF
\((1-2t)^{-1/2},\quad t \lt \tfrac12\)
kth moment
\(E(Y^k)=\dfrac{2^k\,\Gamma(k+\frac12)}{\Gamma(\frac12)}=1\cdot3\cdots(2k-1)\)
Mean
\(1\)
Variance

Key derivation: the CDF method

  1. \(G(y)=P(Z^2\le y)=P(-\sqrt y\le Z\le \sqrt y)=2\Phi(\sqrt y)-1\)

    Squaring is two-to-one. Symmetry gives \(\Phi(-a)=1-\Phi(a)\).

  2. \(g(y)=G'(y)=2\,\phi(\sqrt y)\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt y}=\dfrac{e^{-y/2}\,y^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\)

    Differentiate the CDF (Leibniz rule, chain rule on \(\sqrt y\)).

  3. \(\sqrt{2\pi}=2^{1/2}\,\Gamma(\tfrac12)\)

    Rewrite the constant. Now it is the χ²(n) density with n = 1.

  4. \(E(e^{tZ^2})=\displaystyle\int\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2(1-2t)/2}dz=(1-2t)^{-1/2}\)

    MGF directly: rescale \(s=z\sqrt{1-2t}\), which needs \(t \lt \frac12\).

LinksIt is χ²(n) with n = 1. Because \(Z^2\sim\chi^2(1)\), squaring a t(n) gives an F(1, n).

Add n of them

Chi-square with n degrees of freedom

\(Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\cdots+Z_n^2\sim\chi^2(n),\quad Z_i\ \text{independent}\)

Degrees of freedom count the independent squared Z's. Each one adds 1 to the mean and 2 to the variance, so the hump moves right and spreads out. For large n it is close to N(n, 2n).

Density
\(f(x)=\dfrac{e^{-x/2}\,x^{\frac n2-1}}{2^{n/2}\,\Gamma(\frac n2)},\quad x \gt 0\)
MGF
\((1-2t)^{-n/2},\quad t \lt \tfrac12\)
kth moment
\(E(X^k)=\dfrac{2^k\,\Gamma(\frac n2+k)}{\Gamma(\frac n2)}=n(n+2)\cdots(n+2k-2)\)Valid for any real \(k \gt -\frac n2\), including negative k. The t and F moments depend on this.
Mean
\(n\)
Variance
\(2n\)

Key derivations

  1. \(M(t)=\dfrac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(\frac n2)}\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-x(\frac12-t)}x^{\frac n2-1}dx=\dfrac{1}{2^{n/2}(\frac12-t)^{n/2}}=(1-2t)^{-n/2}\)

    Substitute \(u=x(\frac12-t)\). The integral becomes \(\Gamma(\frac n2)\) and cancels. Needs \(t \lt \frac12\).

  2. \(M'(0)=n,\quad M''(0)=n(n+2)\)

    So \(\operatorname{Var}=n(n+2)-n^2=2n\).

  3. \(\displaystyle\int_0^\infty x^{\frac n2+k-1}e^{-x/2}dx=2^{\frac n2+k}\,\Gamma(\tfrac n2+k)\)

    For \(E(X^k)\), absorb \(x^k\) into the power and use the workhorse with θ = 2.

Its parent: Gamma(α, θ)

\(f(x)=\dfrac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x/\theta}}{\theta^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)}\), MGF \((1-\theta t)^{-\alpha}\), \(E(X^k)=\dfrac{\theta^k\Gamma(\alpha+k)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\), mean \(\alpha\theta\), variance \(\alpha\theta^2\).

Put \(\alpha=\frac n2,\ \theta=2\) and every χ²(n) fact above drops out: mean \(\frac n2\cdot 2=n\), variance \(\frac n2\cdot4=2n\).

LinksAdditivity: if they are independent, \(\chi^2(m)+\chi^2(n)=\chi^2(m+n)\), because the MGFs multiply and the exponents add.

Divide by √(χ²/n)

Student's t with n degrees of freedom

\(T=\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{Y/n}},\quad Y\sim\chi^2(n),\ Z\perp Y\)

This is Z divided by a noisy estimate of 1. Usually the denominator is near 1 and T behaves like Z. Sometimes it is small and T shoots out, which is where the heavy tails come from. As n → ∞ the denominator settles at 1 and t(n) → N(0, 1).

Density
\(f(t)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\sqrt{n\pi}\;\Gamma(\frac n2)}\Big(1+\dfrac{t^2}{n}\Big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt n\,B(\frac12,\frac n2)}\Big(1+\dfrac{t^2}{n}\Big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\)
MGF
Does not exist.The tails shrink like a power, \(|t|^{-(n+1)}\), too slowly for \(E(e^{sT})\) to be finite.
kth moment
\(E(T^k)=n^{k/2}\,E(Z^k)\,E(Y^{-k/2})\): zero for odd k, and for even k \(\ E(T^k)=\dfrac{n^{k/2}\,\Gamma(\frac{k+1}{2})\,\Gamma(\frac{n-k}{2})}{\sqrt\pi\;\Gamma(\frac n2)}\)Exists only when k < n. For example \(E(T^4)=\frac{3n^2}{(n-2)(n-4)}\) for n > 4.
Mean
\(0\quad (n \gt 1)\)
Variance
\(\dfrac{n}{n-2}\quad (n \gt 2)\)Always bigger than 1, and it tends to 1 as n grows.

Key derivation: the Jacobian method

  1. \(f(x_1,x_2)=\phi(x_1)\cdot f_{\chi^2_n}(x_2)\)

    Independence: multiply the N(0,1) and χ²(n) densities.

  2. \(y=\dfrac{x_1}{\sqrt{x_2/n}},\ u=x_2\ \Rightarrow\ x_1=y\sqrt{u/n},\ \ |J|=\sqrt{u/n}\)

    New variable plus a helper, then old in terms of new.

  3. \(\displaystyle\int_0^\infty u^{\frac{n+1}{2}-1}e^{-\frac u2\left(1+\frac{y^2}{n}\right)}du=\Big(\frac{2}{1+y^2/n}\Big)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\big)\)

    Substitute, multiply by \(|J|\) and integrate u out with the workhorse.

  4. \(\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\frac n2)}=\dfrac{1}{B(\frac12,\frac n2)}\)

    Write \(\sqrt\pi=\Gamma(\frac12)\) to get the Beta form.

  5. \(E(Y^{-k/2})=\dfrac{2^{-k/2}\,\Gamma(\frac{n-k}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac n2)}\)

    Moments: independence splits \(E(T^k)\). The χ² formula with k replaced by −k/2 needs n − k > 0.

Linksn = 1 is the Cauchy distribution, which has no mean. \(T^2\sim F(1,n)\). As \(n\to\infty\), \((1+\frac{t^2}{n})^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\to e^{-t^2/2}\). Student's theorem, below, shows where a t comes from in real data.

Divide two χ²'s

F with (n₁, n₂) degrees of freedom

\(F=\dfrac{U/n_1}{V/n_2},\quad U\sim\chi^2(n_1),\ V\sim\chi^2(n_2),\ U\perp V\)

A ratio of two averages of squared Z's. Each average is close to 1, so F hovers around 1, which makes it the tool for comparing two variances. n₁ shapes the curve near 0. n₂ controls the right tail and decides which moments exist.

Density
\(f(x)=\dfrac{\big(\frac{n_1}{n_2}\big)^{n_1/2}\,x^{\frac{n_1}{2}-1}}{B\big(\frac{n_1}{2},\frac{n_2}{2}\big)\big(1+\frac{n_1}{n_2}x\big)^{\frac{n_1+n_2}{2}}},\quad x \gt 0\)
MGF
Does not exist.Power-law right tail, as with t.
kth moment
\(E(X^k)=\Big(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\Big)^k\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac{n_1}{2}+k)\,\Gamma(\frac{n_2}{2}-k)}{\Gamma(\frac{n_1}{2})\,\Gamma(\frac{n_2}{2})}\)Exists only when n₂ > 2k.
Mean
\(\dfrac{n_2}{n_2-2}\quad (n_2 \gt 2)\)Depends only on the denominator df.
Variance
\(\dfrac{2n_2^2\,(n_1+n_2-2)}{n_1(n_2-2)^2(n_2-4)}\quad (n_2 \gt 4)\)

Key derivations

  1. \(u=\dfrac{x_1/n_1}{x_2/n_2},\ v=x_2\ \Rightarrow\ x_1=\dfrac{n_1}{n_2}uv,\ \ |J|=\dfrac{n_1}{n_2}v\)

    The same Jacobian recipe as t, starting from two χ² densities.

  2. \(\dfrac{\Gamma(\frac{n_1+n_2}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n_1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{n_2}{2})}=\dfrac{1}{B(\frac{n_1}{2},\frac{n_2}{2})}\)

    Integrate v out with the workhorse, θ = 2/(1 + n₁u/n₂). The Gamma ratio left over is 1/B.

  3. \(E(X^k)=\big(\tfrac{n_2}{n_1}\big)^kE(U^k)\,E(V^{-k})\)

    Independence again, then the χ² formula with +k and −k. The −k part needs \(\frac{n_2}{2}-k \gt 0\).

  4. \(E(X^2)=\Big(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\Big)^2\dfrac{n_1(n_1+2)}{(n_2-2)(n_2-4)}\)

    k = 1 gives the mean. For the variance, subtract the squared mean over a common denominator.

Links\(T^2\sim F(1,n)\) and \(1/F\sim F(n_2,n_1)\). Mode \(=\frac{n_2}{n_2+2}\cdot\frac{n_1-2}{n_1}\), which is below 1, while the mean is above 1. For large df, \(F\approx N\big(1,\ 2(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2})\big)\).

Student's t theorem

This is where the family pays off. Sample from a Normal population with σ unknown, replace σ by the sample's S, and the standardized mean follows Student's t. The proof shows that the data hand you exactly the two ingredients of step 5: a Z on top and an independent χ² underneath.

The theorem

\[X_1,\dots,X_n\overset{iid}{\sim}N(\mu,\sigma^2),\quad \bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i,\quad S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t(n-1)\]

If σ were known, \((\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\) would be exactly Z. Using S instead adds a second source of randomness, and the result is t with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

The proof in four parts

Running example: 4 heights from N(170, 10²), so μ = 170, σ = 10, n = 4. The sample is 182, 165, 174, 171.

  1. A

    The sample mean is Normal, so it gives a Z

    used in parts C, D
    Start with
    n independent values \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) from \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\).
    Do this
    average them: x̄→subtract μ→divide by σ/√n

    The last two moves are step 1, using x̄'s own standard deviation σ/√n.

    You get
    \(\bar X\sim N\big(\mu,\tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\big)\), so \(Z=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)\)
    With numbers
    x̄ = (182 + 165 + 174 + 171) / 4 = 173. σ/√n = 10 / 2 = 5. Z = (173 − 170) / 5 = 0.6.
    Why
    A sum of independent Normals is Normal, and averaging n of them shrinks the spread by a factor of √n.

    Proved bythe MGF product \(M_{\bar X}(t)=\big[M_X(t/n)\big]^n=e^{\mu t+\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\frac{t^2}{2}}\), which is the N(μ, σ²/n) MGF, then uniqueness. It is a product of n MGFs, not a sum.

  2. B

    x̄ and S² are independent

    used in parts C, D
    Start with
    x̄ and the deviations \(X_i-\bar X\). S² is built only from these deviations.
    Show
    \(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X,\ X_i-\bar X)=\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X,X_i)-\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}=0\)
    Conclude
    x̄ and all the deviations are linear combinations of the same Normal X's, so they are jointly Normal. For jointly Normal variables, zero covariance means independence. So x̄ is independent of every deviation, and therefore of S².
    Why it matters
    Step 5 needs the top and the bottom to be independent. This is the part that really needs Normal data: for other populations, zero covariance does not give independence, and x̄ and S² are dependent.

    Proved by\(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X,X_i)=\frac1n\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\), since only the \(j=i\) term of \(\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_j\) is correlated with \(X_i\).

  3. C

    (n − 1)S²/σ² is chi-square with n − 1 df

    used in part D
    Start with
    The sum of squares around the true mean, \(V=\sum\Big(\dfrac{X_i-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)^2\sim\chi^2(n)\). These are n squared Z's, as in step 3.
    Do this
    add and subtract x̄ inside→expand the square→drop the cross term

    The cross term is 0 because the deviations \(X_i-\bar X\) always add up to 0.

    You get
    \(\underbrace{\sum\Big(\frac{X_i-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)^2}_{V\,\sim\,\chi^2(n)}=\underbrace{\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}}_{U}+\underbrace{\Big(\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\Big)^2}_{Z^2\,\sim\,\chi^2(1)}\)
    With numbers
    From μ = 170 the deviations are 12, −5, 4, 1. Their squares add to 186, so V = 186 / 100 = 1.86.
    From x̄ = 173 the deviations are 9, −8, 1, −2. Their squares add to 150, so U = 150 / 100 = 1.5 (and S² = 150 / 3 = 50).
    From part A, Z² = 0.6² = 0.36. Check: 1.5 + 0.36 = 1.86.
    Finish
    U and Z² are independent by part B, so their MGFs multiply: \((1-2t)^{-n/2}=M_U(t)\,(1-2t)^{-1/2}\), which gives \(M_U(t)=(1-2t)^{-(n-1)/2}\). That is the χ²(n − 1) MGF.
    Why n − 1
    Of the n squares in V, one is spent on how far x̄ missed μ. That leaves n − 1 for S².

    Proved bythe split above, then dividing MGFs and using uniqueness.

  4. D

    Put it together: Student's t with n − 1 df

    Start with
    Z from part A and \(U\sim\chi^2(n-1)\) from part C. They are independent by part B.
    Do this
    divide U by n − 1→take the square root→divide Z by it

    This is exactly the step 5 recipe. Since \(U/(n-1)=S^2/\sigma^2\), σ cancels.

    You get
    \(\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{U/(n-1)}}=\dfrac{(\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}{S/\sigma}=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t(n-1)\)
    With numbers
    U/(n − 1) = 1.5 / 3 = 0.5, √0.5 = 0.707, T = 0.6 / 0.707 = 0.85.
    Straight from the data: S = √50 = 7.07, S/√n = 7.07 / 2 = 3.54, and (173 − 170) / 3.54 = 0.85. Same answer, and σ was never needed.
    Why it matters
    This is the statistic you can compute when σ is unknown. Compare it with t(n − 1), not with Z: for n = 4 the 2.5% point of t(3) is 3.18, while for Z it is 1.96.

    Proved bydividing top and bottom by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), then the definition of t.

Two results that follow directly from part C. \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\) has mean n − 1 and variance 2(n − 1), so \(E(S^2)=\sigma^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S^2)=\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}\). Dividing by n − 1 rather than n is what makes S² average exactly to σ².

One formula behind every moment

Every member of this family is built from Z's and χ²'s, and Z² is itself a χ²(1). So any moment you need breaks down into moments of χ² variables, and those all come from one formula. With the formula, four shortcuts and three moves, you can work out every moment on this page.

The formula

\[Y\sim\chi^2(n):\qquad E\big(Y^{p}\big)=\frac{2^{p}\,\Gamma\big(\frac n2+p\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac n2\big)},\qquad\text{valid when } \tfrac n2+p \gt 0\]

p is the power you raise the χ² to. It can be a positive whole number (1, 2, …), a negative one (−1, −2) or a fraction (such as −½). If \(\frac n2+p\) is not positive, the integral is infinite and that moment does not exist.

Where it comes from: \(E(Y^p)=\int_0^\infty y^p f(y)\,dy\). The \(y^p\) merges into the \(y^{\frac n2-1}\) of the χ² density, and the workhorse integral gives \(2^{\frac n2+p}\,\Gamma(\frac n2+p)\).

The four shortcuts

The Gamma ratio always simplifies with \(\Gamma(a+1)=a\,\Gamma(a)\). These four cases cover almost every exam question.

Power pGamma ratio \(\frac{\Gamma(\frac n2+p)}{\Gamma(\frac n2)}\)Times \(2^p\)So \(E(Y^p)\) isNeeds

The method in three moves

Move 1Rewrite

Write the variable using its recipe from "How each member is made": a Z, some χ²'s and constants.

Move 2Raise and split

Raise it to the power k and pull the constants out. Split independent pieces with \(E(AB)=E(A)\,E(B)\). Treat any \(Z^2\) as a χ²(1).

Move 3Plug in

Each piece is now a χ² to some power p. Read it from the shortcuts, and check \(\frac n2+p \gt 0\) for every piece.

Worked examples

Chi-square(n): mean and variance

  1. Move 1–2
    \(Y\sim\chi^2(n)\)

    It is already a χ², so there is nothing to rewrite or split.

  2. Move 3
    \(E(Y)=n,\qquad E(Y^2)=n(n+2)\)

    The p = 1 and p = 2 shortcuts.

  3. Finish
    \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=n(n+2)-n^2=2n\)

    \(\operatorname{Var}=E(Y^2)-[E(Y)]^2\).

Standard Normal: E(Z⁴), and then any Normal

  1. Move 1
    \(Z^4=\big(Z^2\big)^2,\qquad Z^2\sim\chi^2(1)\)

    An even power of Z is a power of a χ²(1).

  2. Move 3
    \(E(Z^4)=n(n+2)\big|_{n=1}=1\cdot3=3\)

    The p = 2 shortcut with n = 1.

  3. Pattern
    \(E(Z^{2m})=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2m-1)\)

    The same idea with p = m. Odd powers are 0 by symmetry; they are not powers of Z².

  4. Normal X
    \(E\big[(X-\mu)^4\big]=E\big[(\sigma Z)^4\big]=3\sigma^4\)

    \(X-\mu=\sigma Z\), so the constant comes out as \(\sigma^k\).

Student's t(n): variance, then E(T⁴)

  1. Move 1
    \(T=\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{Y/n}}=\sqrt n\;Z\;Y^{-1/2}\)

    Y ~ χ²(n), independent of Z.

  2. Move 2
    \(E(T^2)=n\;E(Z^2)\;E(Y^{-1})\)

    Square it, pull n out, and split by independence.

  3. Move 3
    \(E(Z^2)=1,\qquad E(Y^{-1})=\dfrac{1}{n-2}\)

    Z² is a χ²(1) with p = 1, and Y uses the p = −1 shortcut, which needs n > 2.

  4. Finish
    \(\operatorname{Var}(T)=E(T^2)=\dfrac{n}{n-2}\)

    The mean is 0 because odd powers of Z average to 0.

  5. k = 4
    \(E(T^4)=n^2\cdot E(Z^4)\cdot E(Y^{-2})=n^2\cdot 3\cdot\dfrac{1}{(n-2)(n-4)}=\dfrac{3n^2}{(n-2)(n-4)}\)

    The same moves again, with the p = −2 shortcut, which needs n > 4.

F(n₁, n₂): mean and variance

  1. Move 1
    \(F=\dfrac{U/n_1}{V/n_2}=\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\;U\;V^{-1}\)

    U ~ χ²(n₁) and V ~ χ²(n₂), independent.

  2. Move 2
    \(E(F)=\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}E(U)\,E(V^{-1}),\qquad E(F^2)=\Big(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\Big)^2E(U^2)\,E(V^{-2})\)

    Pull out the constant and split by independence.

  3. Move 3
    \(E(F)=\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\cdot n_1\cdot\dfrac{1}{n_2-2}=\dfrac{n_2}{n_2-2}\)

    p = 1 for U and p = −1 for V. Needs n₂ > 2.

  4. Move 3
    \(E(F^2)=\Big(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\Big)^2\,n_1(n_1+2)\cdot\dfrac{1}{(n_2-2)(n_2-4)}\)

    p = 2 for U and p = −2 for V. Needs n₂ > 4.

  5. Finish
    \(\operatorname{Var}(F)=E(F^2)-[E(F)]^2=\dfrac{2n_2^2(n_1+n_2-2)}{n_1(n_2-2)^2(n_2-4)}\)

    Put both terms over the common denominator \(n_1(n_2-2)^2(n_2-4)\).

Which moments exist

Apply the check \(\frac n2+p \gt 0\) to the χ² piece with the most negative power. Positive powers never cause trouble. Only the χ² you divide by can blow up.

Memberχ² powers used for \(E(X^k)\)Check \(\frac n2+p \gt 0\)So \(E(X^k)\) exists when
Z\(|Z|^k=(\chi^2_1)^{k/2}\)\(\frac12+\frac k2 \gt 0\)always
χ²(n)\(Y^{k}\)\(\frac n2+k \gt 0\)always, for k = 1, 2, 3, …
t(n)\(Y^{-k/2}\) with \(Y\sim\chi^2(n)\)\(\frac n2-\frac k2 \gt 0\)k < n
F(n₁, n₂)\(U^{k}\) and \(V^{-k}\) with \(V\sim\chi^2(n_2)\)\(\frac{n_2}{2}-k \gt 0\)2k < n₂

Why t and F have no MGF. If an MGF exists near 0, every moment exists, because the moments are its Taylor coefficients. t and F have only finitely many moments, so neither has an MGF. On the density side, the t tails fall like \(|t|^{-(n+1)}\), so \(|t|^kf(t)\) is integrable only when k < n.

Outside this family. For a Gamma(α, θ), use the same formula with θ in place of 2 and α in place of n/2: \(E(X^k)=\dfrac{\theta^k\,\Gamma(\alpha+k)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\). The χ² formula is the case α = n/2, θ = 2.

For a distribution that is not built from Normals or Gammas, such as the Uniform or the Poisson, this shortcut does not apply. Use \(E(X^k)=\int x^k f(x)\,dx\), or differentiate the MGF k times at 0: \(E(X^k)=M^{(k)}(0)\).
