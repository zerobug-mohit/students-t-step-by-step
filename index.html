How to read the notation

A formula is a sentence written in shorthand, and you can read it aloud.

Take this line, which appears in chapter 1:

\[ P(a \lt X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

Read it aloud: "The probability that X lands between a and b equals the area under the curve f, from a to b." Whenever a formula is hard to follow, translate each symbol into words using the table below.

Random variables and densities

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

A random variable is a number decided by chance, and its density curve shows where that number tends to land: probability is the area under the curve.

Key terms
Random variable
A number whose value comes from a chance process. "The number on a die", "tomorrow's rainfall", "a person's height".
Discrete
Can only take separate values you could list: 1, 2, 3, ….
Continuous
Can take any value in a range, like 1.7, 1.71, 1.7134….
Density (PDF)
The curve \(f(x)\) for a continuous variable. Areas under it are probabilities.
CDF
\(F(x) = P(X \le x)\): the probability of landing at or below x.
Support
The range of values the variable can actually take, e.g. \(x \ge 0\) for a waiting time.

A discrete example first

Roll a fair die and let \(X\) be the number that shows. \(X\) is a discrete random variable: it can be 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, each with probability \(\tfrac16\). You could draw this as six bars, each of height \(\tfrac16\), and the bars add up to 1.

Now think of a continuous quantity, like the time you wait for a bus. It could be 3 minutes, or 3.2, or 3.217…. There are infinitely many possible values, so the chance of any one exact value (exactly 3.000000… minutes) is zero. Instead we ask about ranges: "what's the chance I wait between 3 and 4 minutes?"

One way to see this is to draw a histogram of many observed values and let the bars get thinner and thinner. The tops of the bars settle onto a smooth curve. That curve is the density.

The density: probability is area

The density \(f(x)\) is a curve that never dips below zero and whose total area is 1. To find the probability that \(X\) lands in a range, take the area under the curve over that range:

\[ P(a \lt X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1. \]

A helpful picture: imagine one kilogram of sand spread along the number line. \(f(x)\) is how high the sand is piled at \(x\). The amount of sand between \(a\) and \(b\) is the probability of landing there.

Common confusion

The height \(f(x)\) is not a probability. It's probability per unit length, like density in physics (kilograms per metre). That's why it can be bigger than 1: if all the sand sits on a stretch only half a unit wide, it must be piled 2 units high to still weigh 1 kg. Only areas are probabilities.

The CDF: a running total

The cumulative distribution function answers "what's the chance of landing at or below x?" It adds up all the sand from the far left up to x:

\[ F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt. \]

(The letter inside the integral is changed to \(t\) only because \(x\) is already being used as the stopping point. Any other letter would work.) As \(x\) moves right, \(F(x)\) climbs from 0 to 1, never going down.

Why the slope of the CDF is the density

Goal: show that \(F'(x) = f(x)\). Chapter 7 uses this: find the CDF first, then differentiate it to get the density.

Plan: nudge x a tiny bit to the right and see how much extra area we pick up.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ F(x+h) - F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x+h} f(t)\,dt - \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt \]

    What we did: wrote down how much the running total grows when we move from x to x + h, where h is tiny.

  2. \[ = \class{hl}{\int_{x}^{x+h} f(t)\,dt} \]

    What we did: the two areas overlap everywhere left of x, so subtracting leaves only the thin strip between x and x + h.

  3. \[ \approx \class{hl}{f(x)\cdot h} \]

    What we did: a very thin strip is almost a rectangle. Its height is \(f(x)\) and its width is h.

    Why is "almost a rectangle" good enough?

    Over a tiny width the curve barely changes height, so the strip's top is nearly flat. The error is much smaller than the strip, and it shrinks faster than h itself. When we divide by h in the next step and let h shrink to zero, that error disappears completely.

  4. \[ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} \approx f(x) \quad\xrightarrow{\;h\to 0\;}\quad \class{hl}{F'(x) = f(x)} \]

    What we did: divided by h. The left side is "rise over run", the slope of F. As h shrinks it becomes exactly the derivative. This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Worked example · waiting for a bus

Say your waiting time in minutes has density \(f(x) = e^{-x}\) for \(x \ge 0\) (the Exponential distribution). What's the chance you wait at most 1 minute?

\[ P(X \le 1) = \int_0^1 e^{-x}\,dx = \Big[-e^{-x}\Big]_0^1 = (-e^{-1}) - (-e^{0}) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632. \]

The general CDF is \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-x}\). Check the slope rule: \(F'(x) = 0 - (-e^{-x}) = e^{-x} = f(x)\). ✓ Pick "Exponential(1)" in the figure above and set b = 1 to see 0.632.

Symmetry of the standard Normal

The standard Normal curve is a mirror image around 0. So the area to the left of \(-a\) equals the area to the right of \(+a\). The area to the right of \(a\) is "everything minus the area to the left", which is \(1 - F(a)\). Therefore:

Symmetry of N(0,1)\[ F(-a) = 1 - F(a) \]

In the figure, choose Normal, untick "start at −∞", and set a = −1, b = 1. The two white tails are equal, each \(1 - F(1) \approx 0.159\).

Recap
  • For continuous variables, probability = area under the density curve. Total area is 1.
  • The density's height is not a probability and can exceed 1.
  • The CDF \(F(x)\) is the area to the left of x. Its slope is the density: \(F' = f\).
  • For N(0,1): \(F(-a) = 1 - F(a)\).

Averages and spread: expectation, variance, moments

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

The expected value is where the distribution balances, and the variance is the average squared distance from that balance point.

Key terms
Expected value \(E(X)\)
The long-run average if you could repeat the experiment forever. Also called the mean, μ.
Variance
The average of the squared distances from the mean. Big variance = widely spread.
Standard deviation σ
The square root of the variance, back in the original units.
k-th moment
\(E(X^k)\): the average of the k-th power. The 1st moment is the mean.
Independent
Two variables that don't affect each other, like two separate dice.

A die example

For a fair die, the expected value is the probability-weighted average of the faces:

\[ E(X) = 1\cdot\tfrac16 + 2\cdot\tfrac16 + 3\cdot\tfrac16 + 4\cdot\tfrac16 + 5\cdot\tfrac16 + 6\cdot\tfrac16 = \tfrac{21}{6} = 3.5. \]

You can never roll 3.5, but if you rolled thousands of times, the average would settle at 3.5. For a continuous variable the recipe is the same, with the sum replaced by an integral and "probability of each value" replaced by \(f(x)\,dx\):

\[ E(X) = \int x\,f(x)\,dx. \]

The same recipe works for any function of X. To average \(g(X)\), weight \(g(x)\) by the density:

Averaging a function of X\[ E\big(g(X)\big) = \int g(x)\,f(x)\,dx \]

This formula is used throughout the guide. Choose \(g(x) = x^k\) and you get the k-th moment \(E(X^k)\). Choose \(g(x) = (x-\mu)^2\) and you get the variance. Choose \(g(x) = e^{tx}\) and you get the MGF of chapter 8.

Worked example · the die's variance

\(E(X^2) = \frac{1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36}{6} = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17\). Using the shortcut derived below, \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 15.17 - 3.5^2 = 15.17 - 12.25 \approx 2.92\), so \(\sigma \approx 1.71\).

The shortcut \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2\)

Goal: a quicker way to compute variance than averaging \((x-\mu)^2\) directly.

Plan: expand the square, then use the fact that averages split over sums.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[(X-\mu)^2\big], \qquad \mu = E(X) \]

    Starting point: the definition. Average the squared distance from the mean.

  2. \[ = E\big[\class{hl}{X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2}\big] \]

    What we did: expanded \((X-\mu)^2\) like \((a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\).

  3. \[ = \class{hl}{E(X^2) - 2\mu\,E(X) + \mu^2} \]

    What we did: split the average over the three terms and pulled constants out.

    Why can we split it like that?

    Expectation is linear: the average of a sum is the sum of the averages, and constants come out front. In symbols \(E(aX + bY + c) = aE(X) + bE(Y) + c\). Here μ is a fixed number, so \(E(2\mu X) = 2\mu E(X)\) and \(E(\mu^2) = \mu^2\) (the average of a constant is that constant).

  4. \[ = E(X^2) - 2\mu\cdot\class{hl}{\mu} + \mu^2 \]

    What we did: replaced \(E(X)\) by μ, since that's what μ means.

  5. \[ = \class{hl}{E(X^2) - \mu^2} \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad E(X^2) = \sigma^2 + \mu^2 \]

    What we did: \(-2\mu^2 + \mu^2 = -\mu^2\). The rearranged version is used in chapter 13.

Three rules for combining variables

Later chapters handle sums and products of random variables constantly. Two dice make each rule concrete:

  • Averages add, always. \(E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)\). Two dice: \(3.5 + 3.5 = 7\). Also \(E(aX + b) = aE(X) + b\).
  • Scaling a variable scales its variance by the square. \(\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\). Doubling every value quadruples the squares. Adding b shifts everything and changes no distances.
  • Independence lets averages of products split. If X and Y are independent, \(E(XY) = E(X)\,E(Y)\), and more generally \(E\big(g(X)\,h(Y)\big) = E(g(X))\,E(h(Y))\). Two separate dice: \(E(XY) = 3.5 \times 3.5 = 12.25\). Independence also gives \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)\). Chapters 11 and 12 use this splitting to find the moments of t and F.
Common confusion

\(E(XY) = E(X)E(Y)\) needs independence. If Y = X (the same die counted twice), \(E(X\cdot X) = E(X^2) \approx 15.17\), not \(12.25\).

The sample mean has mean μ and variance σ²/n

Goal: describe the average of n measurements, \(\bar X\). Chapter 13 relies on this result.

Plan: apply the three rules above, one at a time.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \bar X = \frac1n (X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n), \qquad X_i \text{ iid, each with mean } \mu \text{ and variance } \sigma^2 \]

    Starting point: n independent measurements from the same population, averaged.

  2. \[ E(\bar X) = \frac1n \big(\class{hl}{\mu + \mu + \cdots + \mu}\big) = \frac1n \cdot n\mu = \mu \]

    What we did: averages add, and each \(E(X_i) = \mu\). So the sample mean is centred on the true mean.

  3. \[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \class{hl}{\frac{1}{n^2}}\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) \]

    What we did: the constant \(\frac1n\) comes out of a variance squared.

  4. \[ = \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot \class{hl}{n\sigma^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \]

    What we did: independence, so the variance of the sum is the sum of the n variances.

    What does σ²/n mean in plain words?

    Averaging reduces random variation. With 4 measurements the variance of the average is a quarter of a single measurement's, so its standard deviation is halved (\(\sigma/\sqrt4 = \sigma/2\)). With 100 measurements the standard deviation shrinks by a factor of 10.

  5. \[ E(\bar X^2) = \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) + [E(\bar X)]^2 = \class{hl}{\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2} \]

    What we did: used the shortcut \(E(Y^2) = \operatorname{Var}(Y) + [E(Y)]^2\) with \(Y = \bar X\). Chapter 13 starts from this line.

Recap
  • \(E(g(X)) = \int g(x) f(x)\,dx\). Every mean, moment and MGF is this one recipe.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - \mu^2\), equivalently \(E(X^2) = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\).
  • Averages always add; variances add when independent; \(E(XY) = E(X)E(Y)\) when independent.
  • \(\bar X\) has mean μ and variance \(\sigma^2/n\).

The Normal curve

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

The Normal is the bell curve, and any Normal turns into the standard one, N(0,1), when you measure distance from the mean in units of σ.

Key terms
Normal \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\)
The bell-shaped distribution with centre μ and spread σ. Note the second number is the variance σ², not σ.
Standard Normal Z
The Normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, written \(N(0,1)\). Its CDF is called Φ ("phi").
Standardize, z-score
\(z = (x-\mu)/\sigma\): how many standard deviations x is from the mean.

Reading the formula piece by piece

\[ f(x) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}}_{\text{makes the area 1}}\;\underbrace{e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}_{\text{the bell shape}}, \qquad -\infty \lt x \lt \infty \]
  • The bell shape. \((x-\mu)^2\) is the squared distance from the centre. At \(x = \mu\) it's 0, so the exponential is \(e^0 = 1\), the top of the bell. Further away the exponent becomes more negative and the curve falls towards 0, the same way on both sides.
  • The \(2\sigma^2\) underneath controls how fast it falls. Small σ: narrow, tall bell. Large σ: wide, flat bell.
  • The constant in front is chosen so the total area comes out to exactly 1. Where \(\sqrt{2\pi}\) comes from is the second half of this chapter.
Worked example · heights

Suppose heights are \(N(160, 8^2)\) in centimetres. How unusual is 176 cm? Its z-score is \(z = \frac{176 - 160}{8} = 2\): two standard deviations above the mean. About 95% of the bell lies within ±2σ, so roughly 2.5% of people are taller than 176 cm. Every Normal question can be answered on the single N(0,1) curve this way.

That's why expressions like \(\frac{X_i - \mu_i}{\sigma_i}\) (chapter 9) and \(\frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\) (chapter 13) keep appearing: each one standardizes a Normal into a Z.

Where √(2π) comes from

Without the constant, the bell \(e^{-x^2/2}\) has total area \(I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx\). We need to know I to divide by it. The difficulty is that no standard formula integrates \(e^{-x^2/2}\) directly.

The standard method is to compute \(I \times I\) instead of I. Two copies of the bell, one along x and one along y, multiply into a round 2-D hill. A round hill is easy to measure by slicing it into rings, like the rings of a tree trunk.

Polar coordinates

Any point in the plane can be described by (x, y), or by its distance from the centre, r, and its angle, θ. They're linked by \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), and by Pythagoras \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\). Our hill's height \(e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2} = e^{-r^2/2}\) depends only on r, which is what makes rings work.

\(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi}\)

Goal: find the area \(I\) under \(e^{-x^2/2}\).

Plan: (1) square it to get a 2-D hill, (2) switch to rings, (3) the ring integral turns out to be easy, (4) take the square root.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}dx \;\cdot\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\class{hl}{y}^2/2}d\class{hl}{y} \]

    What we did: wrote I twice. In the second copy we renamed the letter to y. That's allowed: the name of the integration variable doesn't change the area.

  2. \[ I^2 = \class{hl}{\iint} e^{-\class{hl}{(x^2+y^2)}/2}\,dx\,dy \]

    What we did: combined two separate integrals into one double integral over the whole plane, and merged the exponentials (\(e^a e^b = e^{a+b}\)). This is the volume under the round hill.

  3. \[ x^2 + y^2 = \class{hl}{r^2}, \qquad dx\,dy = \class{hl}{r\,dr\,d\theta} \]

    What we did: switched to polar coordinates.

    Where does the extra r come from?

    A tiny polar patch is a bit of ring: its width is \(dr\) and its length along the ring is \(r\,d\theta\) (an arc of angle \(d\theta\) at radius r). So its area is \(r\,dr\,d\theta\), not \(dr\,d\theta\). Patches far from the centre are bigger. This stretch factor is called a Jacobian; chapter 10 is all about it.

  4. \[ I^2 = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-\class{hl}{r^2}/2}\,\class{hl}{r}\,dr\,d\theta \]

    What we did: substituted. r goes from 0 to ∞ (all distances) and θ from 0 to 2π (all the way round).

  5. \[ \int_0^\infty r\,e^{-r^2/2}\,dr = \Big[-e^{-r^2/2}\Big]_0^\infty = \class{hl}{0 - (-1) = 1} \]

    What we did: the inner integral. The r we gained is exactly what makes it easy.

    Show the substitution

    Let \(s = r^2/2\). Then \(ds = r\,dr\), and the integral becomes \(\int_0^\infty e^{-s}ds = \big[-e^{-s}\big]_0^\infty = 0 - (-1) = 1\). Check by differentiating: \(\frac{d}{dr}\big(-e^{-r^2/2}\big) = r e^{-r^2/2}\). ✓

  6. \[ I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \class{hl}{1}\,d\theta = \class{hl}{2\pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \sqrt{2\pi} \approx 2.5066 \]

    What we did: the outer integral measures the full angle, 2π. Take the positive square root (an area is positive). Dividing \(e^{-x^2/2}\) by \(\sqrt{2\pi}\) gives N(0,1) a total area of 1.

Half the bell

\(e^{-u^2/2}\) is an even function: \(f(-u) = f(u)\), the left half mirrors the right. So the area from 0 to ∞ is exactly half the total: \(\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}du = \tfrac12\sqrt{2\pi}\). Chapter 5 needs this half to find \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)\).

Recap
  • \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\): bell centred at μ, width set by σ. \(Z = (X-\mu)/\sigma \sim N(0,1)\).
  • \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx = \sqrt{2\pi}\), found by squaring and using rings.

Four calculus techniques

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

Nearly all the calculus in this guide uses four techniques: integration by parts, substitution, L'Hôpital's rule, and differentiating an integral.

1 · Integration by parts

When to use it: when you need to integrate a product of two different kinds of function, like \(x \cdot e^{-x}\). It replaces your integral with a different one that is usually easier.

Where the formula comes from

Goal: derive \(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\).

Plan: it is the product rule for derivatives, used in reverse.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \frac{d}{dx}(uv) = u'v + uv' \]

    Starting point: the product rule. u and v are any two functions of x.

  2. \[ \class{hl}{uv} = \class{hl}{\int} u'v\,dx + \class{hl}{\int} uv'\,dx \]

    What we did: integrated both sides. Integrating a derivative gives back the original function, so the left side is uv.

  3. \[ \int uv'\,dx = uv - \int u'v\,dx \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \class{hl}{\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du} \]

    What we did: moved one integral to the other side. The short form uses \(dv = v'\,dx\) and \(du = u'\,dx\).

Many courses write the same rule in the "I and II" form: \(\int \text{I}\cdot\text{II}\,dx = \text{I}\int\text{II}\,dx - \int\big(\tfrac{d}{dx}\text{I}\cdot\int\text{II}\,dx\big)dx\). "I" is the function you differentiate (u), "II" the one you integrate (dv). Rule of thumb for choosing: pick as I the function that gets simpler when differentiated. Powers like \(x\) or \(x^{\alpha-1}\) shrink; \(e^{-x}\) is easy to integrate, so it's II.

Worked example · \(\int_0^\infty x e^{-x}dx\)

Choose \(u = x\) (I) and \(dv = e^{-x}dx\) (II). Then \(du = dx\) and \(v = -e^{-x}\).

\[ \int_0^\infty x e^{-x}dx = \Big[x\cdot(-e^{-x})\Big]_0^\infty - \int_0^\infty (-e^{-x})\,dx = \Big[-xe^{-x}\Big]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx \]

The bracket: at x = 0 it's \(-0\cdot 1 = 0\); at x = ∞ it's "∞ × 0", which needs L'Hôpital (technique 3 below) and comes out 0. The remaining integral is \(\big[-e^{-x}\big]_0^\infty = 0 - (-1) = 1\). So the answer is 1. In chapter 5 you'll see this is \(\Gamma(2)\).

2 · Substitution

When to use it: when the integral would look like a familiar one if you renamed part of it. The recipe has three parts, and all three must be done:

  1. Pick the new variable, e.g. \(x = 2u\).
  2. Replace dx. Differentiate: \(dx = 2\,du\). This accounts for stretching the axis.
  3. Move the limits. If x runs from 0 to ∞, so does u = x/2.
Worked example · \(\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x/2}\,dx\)

Put \(x = 2u\), \(dx = 2\,du\), limits stay 0 to ∞: \(\int_0^\infty 2u\,e^{-u}\,2\,du = 4\int_0^\infty u\,e^{-u}du = 4 \times 1 = 4\), using the previous example. Every χ² integral has \(e^{-x/2}\) in it, and this rescaling is how chapter 9 turns it into the \(e^{-u}\) of the Gamma function.

The three substitutions used later

Goal: recognize each one when it appears.

pink = the new pieces created by the substitution

  1. \[ x = \frac{u^2}{2} \;\Rightarrow\; \class{hl}{dx = u\,du}, \qquad x: 0\to\infty \iff u: 0 \to \infty \]

    Where: chapter 5, for \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)\). Why this one: it turns \(e^{-x}\) into \(e^{-u^2/2}\), the Normal bell, whose area we know from chapter 3.

  2. \[ x = 2u \;\Rightarrow\; \class{hl}{dx = 2\,du}, \qquad e^{-x/2} = \class{hl}{e^{-u}} \]

    Where: chapter 9, χ² moments. Why: a pure rescaling that converts \(e^{-x/2}\) into \(e^{-u}\).

  3. \[ u = x\Big(\tfrac12 - t\Big) \;\Rightarrow\; \class{hl}{dx = \frac{du}{\tfrac12 - t}} \]

    Where: chapter 9, the χ² MGF. Why: same rescaling idea, with scale \(1/(\tfrac12 - t)\). It only works if \(\tfrac12 - t \gt 0\), otherwise the limits would flip to −∞. That's why the χ² MGF needs \(t \lt \tfrac12\).

3 · L'Hôpital's rule: exponentials shrink faster than powers grow

When to use it: when a limit comes out as \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\) or \(\frac00\), which on its own tells you nothing. The rule: differentiate the top and the bottom separately and try again.

Example: \(\lim_{x\to\infty} x e^{-x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x}{e^x}\), which is \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\). Differentiate top and bottom: \(\lim \frac{1}{e^x} = 0\). Repeating the rule k times shows \(\frac{x^k}{e^x} \to 0\) for any power k. In words: \(e^{-x}\) goes to 0 faster than any power of x grows. This is why every boundary term in the Gamma chapter is 0.

4 · Differentiating an integral (the Leibniz rule)

When to use it: when y appears in the limits of an integral and you need the derivative with respect to y. Chapter 7 needs \(\frac{d}{dy}\int_{-\infty}^{\sqrt y} f(t)\,dt\). Build it up in three stages:

  1. Simplest case. \(\frac{d}{dy}\int_a^{y} f(t)\,dt = f(y)\). This is the "slope of the CDF is the density" idea from chapter 1.
  2. Upper limit is a function h(y). Then by the chain rule, \(\frac{d}{dy}\int_a^{h(y)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(h(y)\big)\cdot h'(y)\): height at the edge × how fast the edge moves.
  3. Both limits move. Subtract the same thing for the lower limit:
Leibniz integral rule\[ \frac{d}{dy}\int_{g(y)}^{h(y)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(h(y)\big)\,h'(y) - f\big(g(y)\big)\,g'(y) \]
Worked example · the one chapter 7 needs

Upper limit \(h(y) = \sqrt y\), so \(h'(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt y}\). Lower limit \(-\infty\) doesn't depend on y, so \(g'(y) = 0\) and that whole term is 0. Result: \(\frac{d}{dy}\int_{-\infty}^{\sqrt y}\phi(t)\,dt = \phi(\sqrt y)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}\). That's why the lower-limit term drops out in chapter 7.

Recap
  • Parts: \(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\). Differentiate the power, integrate the exponential.
  • Substitution: pick the new variable, replace dx, move the limits. Do all three every time.
  • L'Hôpital: \(x^k e^{-x} \to 0\). Boundary terms with \(e^{-x}\) at ∞ vanish.
  • Leibniz: derivative of an area with a moving edge = height at the edge × speed of the edge.

The Gamma function

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

The Gamma function is the area under \(e^{-x}x^{\alpha-1}\); for whole numbers it's a factorial, \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\), and it also works for in-between values like ½.

Key terms
Factorial n!
\(n! = n \times (n-1) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1\). So \(4! = 24\), and by convention \(0! = 1\).
Gamma function Γ(α)
A smooth function that passes through the factorials and fills in the gaps between them.
Recursion
A rule that gets each value from the one before, like \(n! = n \times (n-1)!\).
Normalizing constant
The number you divide a shape by so its total area becomes 1.

Why the Gamma function is needed

Factorials only make sense for whole numbers: \(3! = 6\), \(4! = 24\). But the χ² density with 3 degrees of freedom needs something like "\((\tfrac32 - 1)!\)", a factorial of ½. The Gamma function makes sense of this by turning the factorial into an area, and an area can be computed for any shape.

\[ \Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,x^{\alpha-1}\,dx, \qquad \alpha \gt 0. \]

Inside the integral, \(x^{\alpha-1}\) grows as x grows, while \(e^{-x}\) shrinks towards 0. From chapter 4, the exponential shrinks faster, so the curve rises and then falls, and its area is finite. That area is \(\Gamma(\alpha)\).

Worked example · Γ(1), Γ(2), Γ(3) by hand

Γ(1): the power is \(x^0 = 1\), so \(\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx = \big[-e^{-x}\big]_0^\infty = 0 - (-1) = 1\).

Γ(2): \(\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x}dx = 1\), the worked example from chapter 4 (parts once, L'Hôpital for the boundary).

Γ(3): \(\int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-x}dx\). Parts with \(u = x^2\), \(dv = e^{-x}dx\): \(= \big[-x^2e^{-x}\big]_0^\infty + 2\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x}dx = 0 + 2\times 1 = 2\).

Notice the pattern: each time, integration by parts pulled the power down by one and left a multiplier behind. That pattern is the recursion.

The recursion \(\Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha-1)\,\Gamma(\alpha-1)\)

Goal: show the pattern from the worked example works for every α.

Plan: integrate by parts once, show the boundary term is zero, and recognize what's left as Gamma again.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty \underbrace{x^{\alpha-1}}_{u\ (\text{I})}\;\underbrace{e^{-x}dx}_{dv\ (\text{II})} \]

    What we did: labelled the two parts. The power is u because differentiating lowers it. \(e^{-x}\) is dv because it's easy to integrate.

  2. \[ du = \class{hl}{(\alpha-1)x^{\alpha-2}}\,dx, \qquad v = \class{hl}{-e^{-x}} \]

    What we did: differentiated u (power rule: bring the power down, reduce it by 1) and integrated dv.

  3. \[ \Gamma(\alpha) = \class{hl}{\Big[-x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}\Big]_0^\infty} \;-\; \int_0^\infty \class{hl}{(-e^{-x})(\alpha-1)x^{\alpha-2}}\,dx \]

    What we did: plugged into \(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\).

  4. \[ \Big[-x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}\Big]_0^\infty = \class{hl}{0 - 0 = 0} \qquad (\alpha \gt 1) \]

    What we did: checked the boundary term at both ends.

    Why is each end zero?

    At ∞: \(x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x} = \frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{e^x} \to 0\) because exponentials shrink faster than powers grow (L'Hôpital, chapter 4). At 0: \(x^{\alpha-1} = 0^{\text{positive}} = 0\) when α > 1, and \(e^{0} = 1\), so the product is 0.

  5. \[ \Gamma(\alpha) = \class{hl}{+(\alpha-1)}\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{\alpha-2}\,dx \]

    What we did: the two minus signs (from the formula and from \(v = -e^{-x}\)) cancel to a plus, and the constant \((\alpha-1)\) comes outside the integral.

  6. \[ \int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{\alpha-2}dx = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{\class{hl}{(\alpha-1)}-1}dx = \class{hl}{\Gamma(\alpha-1)} \;\Rightarrow\; \Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha-1)\,\Gamma(\alpha-1) \]

    What we did: rewrote the power \(\alpha - 2\) as \((\alpha-1) - 1\). Compare with the definition: it's the Gamma integral with \(\alpha - 1\) in place of α.

  7. \[ \Gamma(n) = (n-1)\,\Gamma(n-1) = (n-1)(n-2)\,\Gamma(n-2) = \cdots = (n-1)(n-2)\cdots 1\cdot\Gamma(1) = \class{hl}{(n-1)!} \]

    What we did: applied the rule again and again until we reached \(\Gamma(1) = 1\).

Common confusion

It's \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\), not \(n!\). So \(\Gamma(5) = 4! = 24\). The shift by one is a convention. Equivalently, \(\Gamma(n+1) = n!\).

\(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\)

Goal: find the "factorial of −½", which the χ² density needs for odd degrees of freedom.

Plan: one substitution turns the Gamma integral into half of the Normal bell from chapter 3, whose area we already know.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \Gamma\big(\tfrac12\big) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,x^{\class{hl}{-1/2}}\,dx \]

    What we did: put α = ½ into the definition, so the power is \(\tfrac12 - 1 = -\tfrac12\).

  2. \[ x = \frac{u^2}{2}:\qquad dx = \class{hl}{u\,du},\qquad x^{-1/2} = \class{hl}{\frac{\sqrt2}{u}},\qquad e^{-x} = \class{hl}{e^{-u^2/2}} \]

    What we did: chose a substitution that creates the bell \(e^{-u^2/2}\). Limits: x from 0 to ∞ means u from 0 to ∞.

    Show each piece

    dx: differentiate \(x = \tfrac12u^2\): \(dx = \tfrac12\cdot 2u\,du = u\,du\).

    \(x^{-1/2}\): \(\big(\frac{u^2}{2}\big)^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{u^2/2}} = \frac{1}{u/\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{u}\).

  3. \[ \Gamma\big(\tfrac12\big) = \int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{\class{hl}{u}}\cdot \class{hl}{u}\,du = \class{hl}{\sqrt2}\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}\,du \]

    What we did: substituted all three pieces. The u from dx cancels the u underneath. Only a constant √2 remains outside.

  4. \[ \int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}du = \class{hl}{\tfrac12\sqrt{2\pi}} \]

    What we did: used chapter 3. The full bell has area \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), and from 0 to ∞ is exactly half, because the bell is even.

  5. \[ \Gamma\big(\tfrac12\big) = \sqrt2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2\,\sqrt\pi}{2} = \frac{2\sqrt\pi}{2} = \class{hl}{\sqrt\pi} \approx 1.7725 \]

    What we did: \(\sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt2\sqrt\pi\), and \(\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2 = 2\) cancels the 2 below. You can also write the answer as \(\sqrt\pi\times\) (total area of N(0,1)) \(= \sqrt\pi\times 1\).

  6. \[ \Gamma\big(\tfrac32\big) = \tfrac12\,\Gamma\big(\tfrac12\big) = \class{hl}{\tfrac12\sqrt\pi},\qquad \Gamma\big(\tfrac52\big) = \tfrac32\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi = \class{hl}{\tfrac34\sqrt\pi} \]

    What we did: applied the recursion again. This gives Γ at every half-integer.

The scaled Gamma integral

This rescaled version of Γ is used in most of the derivations in chapters 9, 11 and 12. Recognising it shortens those derivations to a few lines.

Key formula\[ \int_0^\infty x^{a-1}\,e^{-x/\theta}\,dx = \theta^{a}\,\Gamma(a) \qquad (a \gt 0,\ \theta \gt 0) \]
Why this formula holds

Goal: reduce the rescaled integral to plain Γ(a).

Plan: substitute \(x = \theta u\) so the exponential becomes \(e^{-u}\).

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ x = \theta u,\qquad dx = \theta\,du,\qquad e^{-x/\theta} = e^{-u} \]

    What we did: the substitution. Limits stay 0 to ∞ because θ is positive.

  2. \[ \int_0^\infty \class{hl}{(\theta u)}^{a-1}\,\class{hl}{e^{-u}}\,\class{hl}{\theta\,du} \]

    What we did: replaced x, the exponential, and dx.

  3. \[ = \class{hl}{\theta^{a-1}\cdot\theta}\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-u}du = \class{hl}{\theta^{a}}\,\Gamma(a) \]

    What we did: spread the power over the product, \((\theta u)^{a-1} = \theta^{a-1}u^{a-1}\), and combined \(\theta^{a-1}\cdot\theta^1 = \theta^a\). What's left is Γ(a) by definition.

How to recognise it

Look for three things: (1) the integral runs from 0 to ∞, (2) there's a power of the variable, (3) there's \(e^{-(\text{something})\times\text{variable}}\). Then \(a\) = (the power) + 1 and θ = 1 / (the something).

Example: \(\int_0^\infty x^3 e^{-x/2}dx\). Power 3, so a = 4. Something = ½, so θ = 2. Answer: \(2^4\,\Gamma(4) = 16\times 6 = 96\).

Divide the curve by that constant and its area becomes 1. That's the Gamma distribution:

\[ f(x) = \frac{x^{a-1}e^{-x/\theta}}{\theta^a\,\Gamma(a)},\quad x \gt 0. \]

With \(a = \tfrac n2\) and \(\theta = 2\) this is exactly the χ²(n) density from chapter 9. That's why χ² is called a special case of the Gamma distribution.

Recap
  • \(\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{\alpha-1}dx\), an area that exists for every α > 0.
  • \(\Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha-1)\), so \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\).
  • \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\), then \(\Gamma(\tfrac32) = \tfrac12\sqrt\pi\), and so on.
  • Scaled Gamma integral: \(\int_0^\infty x^{a-1}e^{-x/\theta}dx = \theta^a\Gamma(a)\).

The Beta function

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

The Beta function is the area under \(x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\) between 0 and 1, and it can always be computed from Gamma: \(B(\alpha,\beta) = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\).

Gamma lives on the whole half-line from 0 to ∞. Beta is a related integral on the interval from 0 to 1:

\[ B(\alpha,\beta) = \int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,dx, \qquad \alpha \gt 0,\ \beta \gt 0. \]

Here the two factors control opposite ends. \(x^{\alpha-1}\) is small near 0 and controls the left side; \((1-x)^{\beta-1}\) is small near 1 and controls the right side. Raise α and the hump slides right; raise β and it slides left. When α = β the hump is perfectly centred.

Worked example · B(2, 3) two ways

Directly: \(B(2,3) = \int_0^1 x(1-x)^2dx = \int_0^1 (x - 2x^2 + x^3)\,dx = \tfrac12 - \tfrac23 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac{6 - 8 + 3}{12} = \tfrac{1}{12}\).

With the Gamma formula: \(\frac{\Gamma(2)\Gamma(3)}{\Gamma(5)} = \frac{1!\cdot 2!}{4!} = \frac{2}{24} = \frac1{12}\). ✓ Same answer, with much less work when the powers are larger.

Three quick facts

Goal: check symmetry, \(B(1,1) = 1\), and \(B(\tfrac12,\tfrac12) = \pi\).

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ B(\alpha,\beta) \;\overset{x = 1-y}{=}\; \int_{\class{hl}{1}}^{\class{hl}{0}} \class{hl}{(1-y)}^{\alpha-1}\,\class{hl}{y}^{\beta-1}\,\class{hl}{(-dy)} \]

    Symmetry, part 1: substitute \(x = 1 - y\). Then \(1 - x = y\), \(dx = -dy\), and the limits flip: x = 0 means y = 1, x = 1 means y = 0.

  2. \[ = \int_{\class{hl}{0}}^{\class{hl}{1}} y^{\beta-1}(1-y)^{\alpha-1}\,dy = \class{hl}{B(\beta,\alpha)} \]

    Symmetry, part 2: the minus sign from \(-dy\) flips the limits back to 0 → 1. Now the powers have swapped roles: it's the Beta integral with β first.

  3. \[ B(1,1) = \int_0^1 x^0(1-x)^0\,dx = \int_0^1 1\,dx = \class{hl}{1} = \frac{\Gamma(1)\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(2)} = \frac{1\cdot 1}{1} \]

    B(1,1): both powers are 0, so the curve is flat at height 1. The area is a unit square.

  4. \[ B\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big) = \frac{\Gamma(\frac12)\Gamma(\frac12)}{\Gamma(1)} = \frac{\sqrt\pi\cdot\sqrt\pi}{1} = \class{hl}{\pi} \]

    B(½, ½) with the formula, using \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\) from chapter 5.

  5. \[ \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}} \;\overset{x = \sin^2\theta}{=}\; \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\,d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2} \class{hl}{2}\,d\theta = \class{hl}{\pi} \]

    B(½, ½) directly, as a check that doesn't use the formula. Both routes give π.

    Show the trigonometry

    With \(x = \sin^2\theta\): \(1 - x = \cos^2\theta\), so \(\sqrt{x(1-x)} = \sin\theta\cos\theta\). And \(dx = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta\). Limits: x = 0 ↔ θ = 0, x = 1 ↔ θ = π/2. Everything cancels to 2, and \(2\times\frac\pi2 = \pi\).

How Beta relates to Gamma\[ B(\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)} \]

This fact is often stated with "it can be shown that". The proof needs a two-variable change of variables, so it waits until chapter 10, where it's the first worked example of the Jacobian. For now, the figure lets you check it numerically for any α and β.

Why Beta shows up in t and F

When you derive the t and F densities, the constants come out as a ratio of Gamma functions like \(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac12)\Gamma(\frac n2)}\). By the Beta–Gamma formula, that ratio is \(\frac{1}{B(\frac12, \frac n2)}\). Beta is a shorter way to write it.

Recap
  • \(B(\alpha,\beta) = \int_0^1 x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx\).
  • Symmetric: \(B(\alpha,\beta) = B(\beta,\alpha)\). Special values \(B(1,1) = 1\), \(B(\tfrac12,\tfrac12) = \pi\).
  • Compute it with \(B = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\).

Squaring a Normal: χ² with 1 degree of freedom

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

If Z is standard Normal, then Z² follows the χ²(1) distribution, which is concentrated near zero; we find its density by finding its CDF first and then differentiating.

Key terms
Transformation
Making a new random variable from an old one, like \(Y = X^2\).
CDF method
A three-step recipe for the density of a transformed variable (below).
χ²(1)
"Chi-squared with 1 degree of freedom": the distribution of one squared standard Normal.

Some example values

Draw some standard Normal values and square them:

Z0.3−0.81.2−0.12.0−0.5
Z²0.090.641.440.014.000.25

Two things stand out. Squares are never negative. And because most Z's are between −1 and 1, and squaring a number smaller than 1 makes it even smaller, most squares are close to 0. Occasionally a Z of 2 or 3 produces a square of 4 or 9, giving a long tail to the right.

A common mistake

You can't get the density of \(Y = X^2\) by plugging \(\sqrt y\) into the Normal density. Squaring squeezes and stretches the number line, and squeezing sand into a narrower space makes the pile taller. Densities need to account for that. Probabilities, on the other hand, transform simply: the event "Y ≤ y" is literally the same event as "X between −√y and √y". So we work with the CDF (a probability) first.

The CDF method, three steps
  1. Write the CDF of the new variable: \(G(y) = P(Y \le y)\).
  2. Rewrite the event in terms of the old variable, and use its known CDF.
  3. Differentiate \(G(y)\) to get the density \(g(y)\).
The χ²(1) density

Goal: find the density g(y) of \(Y = X^2\), where \(X \sim N(0,1)\).

Plan: the three-step CDF method: (1) write the CDF, (2) turn it into a statement about X, (3) differentiate using the Leibniz rule from chapter 4.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ G_Y(y) = P(Y \le y) = P(\class{hl}{X^2} \le y), \qquad y \gt 0 \]

    Step 1 of the method: the CDF of Y, then replace Y by what it is, \(X^2\).

  2. \[ = P\big(\class{hl}{|X| \le \sqrt y}\big) = P\big(\class{hl}{-\sqrt y \le X \le \sqrt y}\big) \]

    Step 2: rewrite the event in terms of X.

    Why do we get both −√y and +√y?

    Squaring forgets the sign: both 1.5 and −1.5 square to 2.25. So "\(X^2 \le 4\)" holds for every X between −2 and 2. In general \(X^2 \le y\) exactly when \(-\sqrt y \le X \le \sqrt y\).

  3. \[ = \class{hl}{F_X(\sqrt y) - F_X(-\sqrt y)} \]

    What we did: the probability of landing between two points is the CDF at the right point minus the CDF at the left point.

  4. \[ = F_X(\sqrt y) - \class{hl}{\big[1 - F_X(\sqrt y)\big]} = \class{hl}{2F_X(\sqrt y) - 1} \]

    What we did: used the symmetry of N(0,1) from chapter 1, \(F(-a) = 1 - F(a)\), with \(a = \sqrt y\). Then collected terms.

  5. \[ g_Y(y) = \class{hl}{\frac{d}{dy}}\Big[2F_X(\sqrt y) - 1\Big] = 2\,\frac{d}{dy}\class{hl}{\int_{-\infty}^{\sqrt y}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx} \]

    Step 3: density = derivative of CDF. The −1 is a constant, so its derivative is 0. We wrote \(F_X(\sqrt y)\) out as the area it stands for.

  6. \[ = 2\Big[\class{hl}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(\sqrt y)^2/2}\cdot\frac{d}{dy}\sqrt y} \;-\; \class{hl}{0}\Big] \]

    What we did: Leibniz rule: (height at the upper edge) × (speed of the upper edge), minus the same for the lower edge. The lower edge is −∞, which doesn't move with y, so that term is 0.

  7. \[ = 2\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\class{hl}{e^{-y/2}}\cdot\class{hl}{\frac{1}{2}y^{-1/2}} \]

    What we did: simplified the two pieces.

    Show the two simplifications

    \((\sqrt y)^2 = y\), so \(e^{-(\sqrt y)^2/2} = e^{-y/2}\).

    \(\frac{d}{dy}\sqrt y = \frac{d}{dy}y^{1/2} = \frac12 y^{-1/2}\) by the power rule.

  8. \[ g_Y(y) = \class{hl}{\frac{e^{-y/2}\,y^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}}, \qquad y \gt 0 \]

    What we did: the 2 in front and the ½ cancel. This is the blue curve on the right panel above.

  9. \[ \sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt2\cdot\sqrt\pi = \class{hl}{2^{1/2}\,\Gamma(\tfrac12)} \quad\Rightarrow\quad g_Y(y) = \frac{y^{\class{hl}{\frac12 - 1}}\,e^{-y/2}}{2^{1/2}\,\Gamma(\frac12)} \]

    What we did: rewrote the same formula using \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\) and \(y^{-1/2} = y^{\frac12 - 1}\). Why bother? Because now it has exactly the shape of the general χ²(n) formula in chapter 9, with n = 1.

Why the density is very large near 0

The band \(-0.1 \le X \le 0.1\) holds about 8% of N(0,1)'s probability. Squaring sends all of it into the tiny interval \(0 \le Y \le 0.01\). Fitting 8% of the sand into a width of 0.01 makes a very tall pile. The \(y^{-1/2}\) factor (which is huge when y is tiny) measures exactly this squeeze.

Moment generating functions

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

The MGF \(M_X(t) = E(e^{tX})\) is one function that stores all of X's moments, and it turns sums of independent variables into products, which makes it the easiest tool for finding the distribution of a sum.

Key terms
MGF
Moment generating function, \(M_X(t) = E(e^{tX})\). A function of a new variable t.
Taylor series
Writing a function as an endless polynomial, like \(e^u = 1 + u + \frac{u^2}{2!} + \cdots\).
Uniqueness
Two distributions with the same MGF are the same distribution.

Why MGFs are useful

Over and over, the later chapters ask "what is the distribution of a sum of independent variables?" A sum of n squared Normals (chapter 9). An average of n measurements (chapter 13). With densities alone, sums are hard to work with. MGFs make them simpler: the MGF of a sum is the product of the MGFs. Before using that, let's see what an MGF actually is.

Taylor series

The exponential function can be written as an endless polynomial:

\[ e^u = 1 + u + \frac{u^2}{2!} + \frac{u^3}{3!} + \cdots \]

Check with u = 0.5: \(1 + 0.5 + 0.125 + 0.0208 + 0.0026 = 1.6484\), and \(e^{0.5} = 1.6487\). A handful of terms already gets very close.

Put \(u = tX\) and take the average of both sides. Averages split over sums (chapter 2), and each \(t^k\) is a number:

\[ M_X(t) = E(e^{tX}) = 1 + E(X)\,t + E(X^2)\frac{t^2}{2!} + E(X^3)\frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots \]

So the MGF is a polynomial whose coefficients are the moments. That's why it's called a moment generating function: it holds all the moments, and we can pull any one of them out.

Getting the moments from the MGF: \(E(X^k) = M^{(k)}(0)\)

Goal: get E(X) and E(X²) back out of M(t).

Plan: differentiate the series, then set t = 0 so that every term with a t in it vanishes.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ M(t) = 1 + E(X)\,t + E(X^2)\frac{t^2}{2} + E(X^3)\frac{t^3}{6} + \cdots \]

    Starting point: the series (with 2! = 2 and 3! = 6).

  2. \[ M'(t) = \class{hl}{0} + E(X) + E(X^2)\,\class{hl}{t} + E(X^3)\frac{\class{hl}{t^2}}{2} + \cdots \]

    What we did: differentiated each term with the power rule: the constant 1 becomes 0, \(t \to 1\), \(\frac{t^2}{2} \to t\), \(\frac{t^3}{6} \to \frac{t^2}{2}\).

  3. \[ M'(\class{hl}{0}) = E(X) \]

    What we did: set t = 0. Every term still containing t becomes 0. Only E(X) survives.

  4. \[ M''(t) = E(X^2) + E(X^3)\,t + \cdots \quad\Rightarrow\quad \class{hl}{M''(0) = E(X^2)} \]

    What we did: differentiated once more and set t = 0. The pattern continues: the k-th derivative at 0 is the k-th moment. Chapter 9 uses this to get the χ² mean and variance.

Property 1: sums become products

The MGF of a sum

Goal: find \(M_{X+Y}\) when X and Y are independent.

    What we did: the exponent rule \(e^{a+b} = e^a e^b\).

    What we did: because X and Y are independent, the average of the product splits into the product of averages (chapter 2).

    Also, the scaling rule: \(e^{bt}\) is a constant and comes out; the rest is the MGF of X evaluated at \(at\) instead of t.

Property 2: the MGF identifies the distribution

Uniqueness: if two random variables have the same MGF (on some interval around 0), they have the same distribution. So to prove "\(\bar X\) is Normal", it's enough to compute its MGF and recognize the Normal's formula. That's the strategy in the sample-mean derivation below, and again in chapter 13.

The Normal's MGF, by completing the square

    What we did: matched it to the Normal MGF, with σ² replaced by σ²/n. By uniqueness, \(\bar X\) is Normal.

A common mistake

χ² with n degrees of freedom

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

Add up n independent squared standard Normals and you get χ²(n): its average is n, its variance is 2n, and its density is a Gamma curve.

Key terms
Degrees of freedom (df)
Here, the number of independent squared Z's that were added up. Written n.
χ²(n)
The distribution of \(Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \cdots + Z_n^2\) with independent \(Z_i \sim N(0,1)\).
Additivity
Adding independent χ² variables adds their degrees of freedom.
\[ \chi^2 = \Big(\frac{X_1-\mu_1}{\sigma_1}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{X_2-\mu_2}{\sigma_2}\Big)^2 + \cdots + \Big(\frac{X_n-\mu_n}{\sigma_n}\Big)^2 \sim \chi^2(n) \]

That's the definition. Each bracket standardizes a Normal into a Z (chapter 3), so it really is a sum of n squared Z's.

What to expect before any algebra: each \(Z^2\) has average \(E(Z^2) = \operatorname{Var}(Z) + [E(Z)]^2 = 1 + 0 = 1\). Add n of them and the average is n. So the distribution slides right as n grows. And since sums of many independent pieces become bell-shaped (the central limit theorem), χ²(n) looks more and more like a Normal for large n.

Density of χ²(n)\[ f(x) = \frac{e^{-x/2}\,x^{\frac n2 - 1}}{2^{n/2}\,\Gamma(\frac n2)}, \qquad 0 \le x \lt \infty \]
  • \(x^{\frac n2 - 1}\) controls the left side. For n = 1 the power is −½ and the curve goes to infinity at 0 (chapter 7). For n = 2 the power is 0, so the curve starts at a flat ½. For n ≥ 3 the power is positive and the curve starts at 0.
  • \(e^{-x/2}\) pulls the right side down to 0.
  • The constant \(2^{n/2}\Gamma(\frac n2)\) is exactly the scaled Gamma integral \(\int_0^\infty x^{\frac n2-1}e^{-x/2}dx\) with \(a = \tfrac n2\), θ = 2. Dividing by the area makes the area 1. That is where the constant comes from.

Why this density? The MGF route

Here's the logic in three sentences. Each \(Z_i^2\) has MGF \((1-2t)^{-1/2}\) (chapter 8). They're independent, so their sum has MGF \(\big[(1-2t)^{-1/2}\big]^n = (1-2t)^{-n/2}\). And the density in the box above also has MGF \((1-2t)^{-n/2}\) (derivation below), so by uniqueness it must be the density of the sum.

Changing variables: the Jacobian

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

When you change variables, probability must be conserved, so the density gets multiplied by the Jacobian: the factor by which the change stretches tiny lengths or areas.

Key terms
Joint density
\(f(x_1,x_2)\): a surface over the plane. Probability is the volume under it over a region.
Marginal density
The density of one variable alone, found by integrating the other one out.
Determinant
A number computed from a square grid of numbers: \(\det\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix} = ad - bc\).
Jacobian
The determinant of the grid of partial derivatives. It measures how much a change of variables stretches area.

One dimension first

Let \(X \sim N(0,1)\) and make a new variable \(Y = aX\) for some a > 1. Every value gets pushed a times further from 0. A thin strip of X-values of width dx becomes a strip of Y-values of width a·dx. The same probability (the same amount of sand) now sits on a strip a times wider, so the pile must be a times lower.

In symbols: \(f_Y(y) = f_X(x)\cdot\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right|\), where x is written in terms of y. Here \(x = y/a\), so \(\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac1a\). That factor \(\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right|\) is a one-dimensional Jacobian. It's the same stretch factor as in substitution (chapter 4).

Two variables at once

t and F are built from two random variables, so we need the 2-D version. Three facts first:

  • Independence means the joint density factors: \(f(x_1,x_2) = f_1(x_1)\,f_2(x_2)\). That's how the t and F derivations start: multiply the two densities.
  • Marginal: to get the density of one variable, integrate the other out: \(f_1(x_1) = \int f(x_1,x_2)\,dx_2\). This is the "integrate the helper out" step at the end of the t and F derivations.
  • Change of variables: exactly the 1-D idea, with "length stretch" replaced by "area stretch":
Change of variables in two dimensions\[ g(y,u) = f\big(x_1(y,u),\,x_2(y,u)\big)\cdot|J|, \qquad J = \det\begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial x_2}{\partial y} \\[2pt] \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x_2}{\partial u}\end{pmatrix} \]
Reading the Jacobian grid

\(\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial y}\) ("partial x₁ by partial y") means: differentiate \(x_1\) with respect to y, treating u as a constant. Each entry of the grid is one such partial derivative. Then the determinant is (top-left × bottom-right) − (top-right × bottom-left).

Example: \(\det\begin{pmatrix}3 & 0\\ 5 & 2\end{pmatrix} = 3\times 2 - 0\times 5 = 6\). Whenever one off-diagonal entry is 0, as in both the t and F examples, the determinant is the product of the diagonal.

The five-step recipe for t and F
  1. Joint density of the old variables. Independent, so multiply.
  2. Choose new variables. The one you want, plus a helper (like u = x₂) so you have two new for two old.
  3. Solve for the old ones in terms of the new ("old in terms of new"), and work out the new ranges.
  4. Jacobian: the determinant of partial derivatives.
  5. Substitute, multiply by |J|, then integrate the helper out.
The proof promised in chapter 6: \(B(\alpha,\beta) = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\)

Goal: connect Beta to Gamma.

Plan: multiply two Gamma integrals into one double integral (like chapter 3), change variables to "total" and "share", and it splits into a Gamma times a Beta.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{\alpha-1}dx\int_0^\infty e^{-y}y^{\beta-1}dy = \class{hl}{\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-(x+y)}\,x^{\alpha-1}y^{\beta-1}\,dx\,dy} \]

    Recipe step 1: two separate integrals combine into one double integral; the exponentials merge.

  2. \[ \class{hl}{u = x + y} \in (0,\infty), \qquad \class{hl}{v = \frac{x}{x+y}} \in (0,1) \]

    Recipe step 2: u is the total, v is the share of the total that came from x. A share is always between 0 and 1.

  3. \[ x = \class{hl}{uv}, \qquad y = \class{hl}{u(1-v)} \]

    Recipe step 3: old in terms of new. If the total is u and x's share is v, then x = uv and y is the rest.

  4. \[ J = \det\begin{pmatrix} \partial x/\partial u & \partial y/\partial u \\ \partial x/\partial v & \partial y/\partial v \end{pmatrix} = \det\begin{pmatrix} \class{hl}{v} & \class{hl}{1-v} \\ \class{hl}{u} & \class{hl}{-u} \end{pmatrix} = -uv - u(1-v) = \class{hl}{-u} \]

    Recipe step 4: four partial derivatives, then ad − bc. Expanding: \(-uv - u + uv = -u\). So \(|J| = u\).

  5. \[ = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-\class{hl}{u}}\,\class{hl}{(uv)}^{\alpha-1}\,\class{hl}{\big(u(1-v)\big)}^{\beta-1}\,\class{hl}{u}\,du\,dv \]

    Recipe step 5: substituted. Note \(x + y = u\), so the exponential only involves u. The last u is |J|.

  6. \[ = \int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,u^{\class{hl}{\alpha+\beta-1}}\,du\;\cdot\;\int_0^1 v^{\alpha-1}(1-v)^{\beta-1}\,dv \]

    What we did: spread the powers and collected all the u's: \((\alpha - 1) + (\beta - 1) + 1 = \alpha + \beta - 1\). Now nothing mixes u and v, so the double integral separates into two single ones.

  7. \[ \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta) = \class{hl}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}\cdot\class{hl}{B(\alpha,\beta)} \;\Rightarrow\; B(\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)} \]

    What we did: recognized both pieces from their definitions and divided. Done.

Recap

  • In 1-D the factor is \(|dx/dy|\); in 2-D it's |J|, a determinant of partial derivatives.
  • Independent ⇒ joint density = product. Integrate a variable out to get a marginal.

The t distribution

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

t is a standard Normal divided by a random number that is usually close to 1. Because that number is sometimes small, t has heavier tails than the Normal, and it approaches the Normal as n grows.

Key terms
t(n)
"t with n degrees of freedom." The n comes from the χ²(n) in the denominator.
Heavy tails
Extreme values (far from 0) happen more often than they would for a Normal.
Cauchy
The special case t(1). Its tails are so heavy it has no mean at all.
\[ T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/n}}, \qquad Z \sim N(0,1),\quad V \sim \chi^2(n),\quad Z \text{ and } V \text{ independent.} \]

An example

Suppose Z came out as 1.5. Now divide it by a few possible values of the denominator \(D = \sqrt{V/n}\):

Denominator D1.11.00.90.60.4
T = 1.5 / D1.361.501.672.503.75

When D is near 1, T is close to Z. But when D is small, T becomes large. These occasional large values make the tails of t heavier.

Why is D usually close to 1? \(V/n\) is an average of n squared Z's (chapter 9), and each averages 1. Its variance is \(\frac{2n}{n^2} = \frac2n\), which shrinks as n grows. So with many degrees of freedom D is almost exactly 1 and T is almost exactly Z. With few degrees of freedom D varies a lot.

Density of t(n)\[ f(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt n\;B\big(\frac12,\frac n2\big)}\Big(1+\frac{t^2}{n}\Big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}, \qquad -\infty \lt t \lt \infty \]
  • \(\big(1+\frac{t^2}{n}\big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\) is the shape. It depends on \(t^2\), so the curve is symmetric around 0, like the Normal.
  • For large t it shrinks like a power, \(|t|^{-(n+1)}\). The Normal's \(e^{-t^2/2}\) shrinks much faster. That's the heavy-tail difference.
  • The constant \(\frac{1}{\sqrt n\,B(\frac12,\frac n2)}\) makes the area 1.
The t density

Goal: the density of \(Y = X_1/\sqrt{X_2/n}\), with \(X_1 \sim N(0,1)\) and \(X_2 \sim \chi^2(n)\) independent.

Plan: the five-step Jacobian recipe from chapter 10, finishing with the scaled Gamma integral and the Beta–Gamma formula.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ f(x_1,x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x_1^2/2}\cdot\frac{e^{-x_2/2}x_2^{\frac n2-1}}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(\frac n2)}, \qquad -\infty\lt x_1\lt\infty,\ 0\lt x_2\lt\infty \]

    Recipe step 1: independent, so the joint density is the N(0,1) density times the χ²(n) density.

  2. \[ \class{hl}{y = \frac{x_1}{\sqrt{x_2/n}}},\quad \class{hl}{u = x_2} \]

    Recipe step 2: y is the variable we want. u is a helper: we need two new variables to replace two old ones, and \(x_2\) itself is the simplest choice.

  3. \[ x_1 = \class{hl}{y\sqrt{u/n}},\qquad x_2 = \class{hl}{u}, \qquad -\infty \lt y \lt \infty,\ 0 \lt u \lt \infty \]

    Recipe step 3: old in terms of new. Multiply both sides of the y equation by \(\sqrt{x_2/n}\) and replace \(x_2\) by u.

  4. \[ J = \det\begin{pmatrix}\partial x_1/\partial y & \partial x_2/\partial y\\ \partial x_1/\partial u & \partial x_2/\partial u\end{pmatrix} = \det\begin{pmatrix}\class{hl}{\sqrt{u/n}} & \class{hl}{0}\\ \class{hl}{\frac{y}{2\sqrt{nu}}} & \class{hl}{1}\end{pmatrix} = \class{hl}{\sqrt{\frac un}} \]

    Recipe step 4: \(x_2 = u\) doesn't involve y, so \(\partial x_2/\partial y = 0\); then the determinant is the product of the diagonal.

  5. \[ g(y,u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\class{hl}{\frac{y^2u}{2n}}}\cdot\frac{e^{-u/2}u^{\frac n2-1}}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(\frac n2)}\cdot\class{hl}{\sqrt{\frac un}} \]

    Recipe step 5a: substituted \(x_1 = y\sqrt{u/n}\) (so \(x_1^2 = \frac{y^2u}{n}\)) and \(x_2 = u\), then multiplied by |J|.

  6. \[ g(y,u) = \frac{1}{\class{hl}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\;2^{n/2}\,\Gamma(\frac n2)}\;e^{\class{hl}{-\frac u2\left(1+\frac{y^2}{n}\right)}}\;u^{\class{hl}{\frac{n+1}{2}-1}} \]

    What we did: tidied up. Now it's the joint density of y and u.

    Show the three simplifications

    Constants: \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\) (the \(\sqrt n\) is from \(\sqrt{u/n}\)).

    Exponentials: \(e^{-\frac{y^2u}{2n}}e^{-\frac u2} = e^{-\frac u2\cdot\frac{y^2}{n} - \frac u2} = e^{-\frac u2(1 + \frac{y^2}{n})}\).

    Powers of u: \(u^{\frac n2 - 1}\cdot u^{1/2} = u^{\frac n2 - \frac12} = u^{\frac{n+1}{2} - 1}\).

  7. \[ g(y) = \int_0^\infty g(y,u)\,du = C\int_0^\infty u^{\frac{n+1}{2}-1}e^{-u/\class{hl}{\theta}}\,du, \qquad \class{hl}{\theta = \frac{2}{1+y^2/n}} \]

    Recipe step 5b: integrate the helper u out, with C the constant in front. Writing \(\frac u2(1+\frac{y^2}{n}) = u/\theta\) shows this is the scaled Gamma integral. (Substituting \(v = \frac u2(1+\frac{y^2}{n})\) by hand does the same thing.)

  8. \[ \int_0^\infty u^{\frac{n+1}{2}-1}e^{-u/\theta}du = \class{hl}{\theta^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,\Gamma\Big(\frac{n+1}{2}\Big)} = \frac{2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\big(1+\frac{y^2}{n}\big)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}} \]

    What we did: used the scaled Gamma integral with \(a = \frac{n+1}{2}\), then wrote out \(\theta^a\).

  9. \[ g(y) = \class{hl}{\frac{2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\;2^{\frac n2}}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac n2)}\Big(1+\frac{y^2}{n}\Big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}} = \frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\class{hl}{\sqrt{n\pi}}\;\Gamma(\frac n2)}\Big(1+\frac{y^2}{n}\Big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}} \]

    What we did: multiplied C by the integral and simplified the powers of 2.

    Show the powers of 2

    \(\frac{2^{(n+1)/2}}{2^{n/2}} = 2^{\frac{n+1}{2} - \frac n2} = 2^{1/2} = \sqrt2\). Then \(\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt{2\pi n}} = \frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2\sqrt{\pi n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\).

  10. \[ \frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\frac n2)} = \frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\class{hl}{\Gamma(\frac12)}\Gamma(\frac n2)} = \class{hl}{\frac{1}{B(\frac12,\frac n2)}} \;\Rightarrow\; g(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt n\,B(\frac12,\frac n2)}\Big(1+\frac{y^2}{n}\Big)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}} \]

    What we did: replaced √π by \(\Gamma(\frac12)\), then used the Beta–Gamma formula \(B(a,b) = \frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\) with \(a = \frac12\), \(b = \frac n2\) (so \(a + b = \frac{n+1}{2}\)).

A common mistake

It's easy to write \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\) in front of the Normal part of the joint density. It should be \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\). Together with the \(\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\) from the Jacobian, that gives the \(\sqrt{2\pi n}\) above.

Moments, and when they exist

A moment \(E(T^k)\) is an area: \(\int t^k f(t)\,dt\). If the tails are heavy enough, that area is infinite and the moment doesn't exist. Higher k makes \(t^k\) bigger in the tails, so higher moments fail first.

\(E(T^k)\) and the variance

Goal: a formula for \(E(T^k)\), and when it exists.

Plan: split T into its two independent pieces, average each, and use the χ² moment formula with a negative power.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ T^k = \frac{W^k}{(Y/n)^{k/2}} = \class{hl}{n^{k/2}\,W^k\,Y^{-k/2}} \]

    What we did: wrote \(T = W/\sqrt{Y/n}\) (W ~ N(0,1), Y ~ χ²(n), independent) and raised it to the power k. Dividing by \((Y/n)^{k/2}\) is multiplying by \(n^{k/2}Y^{-k/2}\).

    What we did: W and Y are independent, so the average of the product splits.

  3. \[ k \text{ odd}: \quad E(W^k) = \class{hl}{0} \;\Rightarrow\; E(T^k) = 0 \quad(\text{if it exists}) \]

    What we did: N(0,1) is symmetric, so positive and negative odd powers cancel out on average.

    What we did: the mean is 0, so variance = \(E(T^2)\). It's always bigger than the Normal's 1, and shrinks towards 1 as n grows.

The same condition, read off the tails

For large |t|, \(|t|^k f(t)\) behaves like \(|t|^{k}\cdot|t|^{-(n+1)} = |t|^{k-n-1}\). An area like \(\int_1^\infty t^{p}\,dt\) is finite only when \(p \lt -1\). So we need \(k - n - 1 \lt -1\), i.e. \(k \lt n\). In words: E(T) exists only if n > 1, E(T²) only if n > 2, and so on.

The F distribution

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

F is the ratio of two independent averages of squared Normals. Each average is close to 1, so F is close to 1, which makes F the standard tool for comparing two variances.

Key terms
F(n₁, n₂)
The F distribution with n₁ "numerator" and n₂ "denominator" degrees of freedom. Order matters.
Variance ratio
One measure of spread divided by another. Near 1 means "similar spread".
\[ F = \frac{U/n_1}{V/n_2}, \qquad U \sim \chi^2(n_1),\ V\sim\chi^2(n_2)\ \text{independent}. \]

Where you'd use it: two machines fill bottles, and you want to know whether one is more variable than the other. Each sample's variance, suitably scaled, behaves like a χ² divided by its degrees of freedom. If the machines are equally consistent, their ratio follows F, so a ratio far from 1 is evidence they differ.

What to expect: \(U/n_1\) and \(V/n_2\) each average 1 (chapter 9). So F is centred near 1. It can't be negative (it's a ratio of positive things), and it has a long right tail because the denominator is sometimes small.

    Expand: \((n_1n_2 - 2n_1 + 2n_2 - 4) - (n_1n_2 - 4n_1) = 2n_1 + 2n_2 - 4 = 2(n_1+n_2-2)\).

Worked example · F(5, 10)

Mean \(\frac{10}{8} = 1.25\). Mode \(\frac{10}{12}\cdot\frac{3}{5} = 0.5\). Variance \(\frac{2\cdot 100\cdot 13}{5\cdot 64\cdot 6} = \frac{2600}{1920} \approx 1.35\). Moments \(E(X^k)\) exist for \(2k \lt 10\), i.e. k = 1, 2, 3, 4. These are the default settings in the figure, so you can check every number there.

More properties and two useful relationships

  • Mode \(= \frac{n_2}{n_2+2}\cdot\frac{n_1-2}{n_1}\) for \(n_1 \gt 2\). Found by setting the derivative of \(\log f\) to zero and solving for x.
  • Large n₁ and n₂: \(F \approx N\big(1,\ 2(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2})\big)\). Each \(\chi^2(n)/n\) is roughly \(N(1, \frac2n)\); dividing two numbers close to 1 roughly adds their variances.
  • t² is F: if \(T \sim t(n)\), then \(T^2 = \frac{Z^2/1}{V/n} \sim F(1,n)\), because \(Z^2 \sim \chi^2(1)\).
  • Flip it: \(1/F \sim F(n_2, n_1)\). Swapping top and bottom swaps the degrees of freedom.
Recap
  • \(F = \frac{U/n_1}{V/n_2}\), centred near 1, never negative.
  • Density via the same Jacobian recipe as t, ending in a Beta function.
  • Mean \(\frac{n_2}{n_2-2}\) (n₂ > 2); variance needs n₂ > 4; \(E(X^k)\) needs n₂ > 2k.
  • \(t(n)^2 = F(1,n)\) and \(1/F(n_1,n_2) = F(n_2,n_1)\).

Why \((\bar X - \mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\) follows t with n − 1 degrees of freedom

BackgroundΓBχ²₁MGFχ²ₙJacobiantFStudent

If you knew σ, \((\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\) would be exactly Normal; using the sample's own S instead adds a little extra randomness, and the result follows t with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

Key terms
Population vs sample
The population has true values μ and σ we don't know. The sample is the n values we actually measured.
Sample mean \(\bar X\)
\(\frac1n\sum X_i\), our estimate of μ.
Sample variance \(S^2\)
\(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2\), our estimate of σ². Note the n − 1.
Covariance
\(\operatorname{Cov}(A,B) = E[(A - EA)(B - EB)]\). Positive if A and B tend to be big together; 0 if there's no linear link.
Jointly Normal
Variables that are all built as sums of the same independent Normals. For them, covariance 0 means fully independent.

Why this matters

Worked example · are the bottles under-filled?

A machine should fill 500 ml. You measure n = 4 bottles: \(\bar x = 495\), \(s = 4\). Is that evidence of under-filling, or random variation?

Standardize: \(\frac{\bar x - 500}{s/\sqrt n} = \frac{495 - 500}{4/2} = -2.5\).

If you (wrongly) compared −2.5 with the Normal's 5% cutoff of ±1.96, you'd conclude the machine is faulty. But with σ estimated from only 4 values, the right yardstick is t(3), whose 5% cutoff is ±3.18. −2.5 is well within it: not enough evidence. The difference between those two answers is what this chapter proves.

Student's theorem \[ X_1,\dots,X_n \overset{iid}{\sim} N(\mu,\sigma^2),\quad \bar X = \frac1n\sum X_i,\quad S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt n} \sim t(n-1) \]

Recall from chapter 11 that a t variable is (standard Normal) ÷ √(χ² ÷ its df), with top and bottom independent. So the proof has to supply three ingredients, and the four steps below do exactly that:

t needs…We'll show…Step

Step 1 · \(\bar X\) is Normal

Already done in chapter 8: \(M_{\bar X}(t) = [M_X(t/n)]^n = e^{\mu t + \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\frac{t^2}{2}}\), the MGF of N(μ, σ²/n). Standardizing gives the Z on top.

Step 2 · \(\bar X\) and \(S^2\) are independent

This step is less obvious. \(\bar X\) and S² are computed from the same data, so why wouldn't they be related? For Normal data they really aren't, and for most other populations they are. The plot below shows this before the proof.

\(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X,\ X_i - \bar X) = 0\)

Goal: show \(\bar X\) has no linear link with any deviation \(X_i - \bar X\). Since S² is built only from those deviations, that's the heart of step 2.

Plan: expand the covariance, then compute \(E(\bar X X_i)\) and \(E(\bar X^2)\) separately and watch them cancel.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \operatorname{Cov}(\bar X, X_i - \bar X) = E\big[(\bar X - \class{hl}{\mu})(X_i - \bar X - \class{hl}{0})\big] \]

    What we did: the definition \(E[(A - EA)(B - EB)]\). Here \(E(\bar X) = \mu\), and \(E(X_i - \bar X) = \mu - \mu = 0\).

  2. \[ = E\big[\class{hl}{\bar X X_i - \bar X^2 - \mu X_i + \mu\bar X}\big] \]

    What we did: multiplied out the brackets.

  3. \[ = E(\bar X X_i) - E(\bar X^2) \class{hl}{- \mu^2 + \mu^2} = E(\bar X X_i) - E(\bar X^2) \]

    What we did: split the average (linearity). \(E(\mu X_i) = \mu\cdot\mu\) and \(E(\mu\bar X) = \mu\cdot\mu\), which cancel.

  4. \[ E(\bar X^2) = \class{hl}{\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2} \]

    What we did: chapter 2's result: \(E(\bar X^2) = \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) + [E(\bar X)]^2\).

  5. \[ E(\bar X X_i) = \frac1n E\Big(\sum_{j} X_j X_i\Big) = \frac1n\Big[\class{hl}{\sum_{j\ne i}E(X_j)E(X_i)} + \class{hl}{E(X_i^2)}\Big] \]

    What we did: wrote \(\bar X = \frac1n\sum_j X_j\) and multiplied by \(X_i\). One term in the sum has \(j = i\), giving \(X_i^2\). The other n − 1 terms pair \(X_i\) with a different, independent \(X_j\), so their averages split.

    See it with n = 3 and i = 1

    \(\bar X X_1 = \frac13(X_1 + X_2 + X_3)X_1 = \frac13(X_1^2 + X_2X_1 + X_3X_1)\). Average: \(\frac13\big(E(X_1^2) + E(X_2)E(X_1) + E(X_3)E(X_1)\big) = \frac13\big((\mu^2+\sigma^2) + \mu^2 + \mu^2\big)\).

  6. \[ = \frac1n\Big[\class{hl}{(n-1)\mu^2} + \class{hl}{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}\Big] = \frac1n\big[n\mu^2 + \sigma^2\big] = \mu^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \]

    What we did: each of the n − 1 cross terms is \(\mu\cdot\mu\), and \(E(X_i^2) = \mu^2 + \sigma^2\) (the shortcut again, from chapter 2).

  7. \[ \operatorname{Cov}(\bar X, X_i - \bar X) = \Big(\mu^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big) - \Big(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\Big) = \class{hl}{0} \]

    What we did: the two pieces are identical, so they cancel.

  8. \[ \text{jointly Normal} + \text{Cov} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \bar X \perp\!\!\!\perp (X_1-\bar X,\dots,X_n - \bar X) \;\Rightarrow\; \class{hl}{\bar X \perp\!\!\!\perp S^2} \]

    What we did: upgraded "zero covariance" to "independent". That's allowed only because the data are Normal (see the box below). S² is made only from the deviations, so it's independent of \(\bar X\) too.

Zero covariance is not enough on its own

You can't go straight from "covariance = 0" to "independent" in general: zero covariance only rules out a straight-line relationship (the Uniform cloud above has a curved one). It works here because \(\bar X\) and every \(X_i - \bar X\) are built by adding up the same independent Normal values, which makes them jointly Normal, and for jointly Normal variables zero covariance does mean independence. This is the justification step 2 needs.

Step 3 · \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\)

The idea is like Pythagoras. The total squared distance of the data from the true mean μ splits exactly into two parts: squared distances from the sample mean \(\bar X\), plus one extra square for how far \(\bar X\) missed μ.

Why n − 1? Degrees of freedom in plain words

Take the sample 4, 7, 10. Its mean is 7, so the deviations are −3, 0, +3. They add up to 0, and that's always true (shown below). So if I tell you two of the deviations, you can work out the third yourself. Only n − 1 of the deviations are "free"; the last is forced.

That's what "one degree of freedom is used up by estimating μ with \(\bar X\)" means. The deviations from \(\bar X\) carry only n − 1 independent squared Normals' worth of information. It's also why S² divides by n − 1: then its average is exactly σ².

The χ²(n − 1) result

Goal: show \(U = \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\) is χ²(n − 1).

Plan: (1) split V = U + W, (2) recognize V as χ²(n) and W as χ²(1), (3) use MGFs to "subtract" one degree of freedom.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ V = \sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma}\Big)^2 \sim \chi^2(n) \]

    Starting point: each bracket is a standard Normal (chapter 3), and there are n independent ones, so V is χ²(n) (chapter 9).

  2. \[ X_i - \mu = \class{hl}{(X_i - \bar X) + (\bar X - \mu)} \]

    What we did: subtracted \(\bar X\) and added it back. Nothing changes, but now we can split.

  3. \[ V = \sum\Big(\frac{X_i-\bar X}{\sigma}\Big)^2 + \class{hl}{n}\Big(\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)^2 + \class{hl}{\frac{2(\bar X - \mu)}{\sigma^2}\sum(X_i - \bar X)} \]

    What we did: squared using \((a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\), then summed over i.

    Where do the n and the cross term come from?

    The middle piece \(\big(\frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma}\big)^2\) is the same for every i, so summing n copies gives n times it.

    The cross term is \(\sum 2\cdot\frac{X_i - \bar X}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma}\). The factor \((\bar X - \mu)\) doesn't depend on i, so it comes outside the sum.

  4. \[ \sum_{i=1}^n(X_i - \bar X) = \sum X_i - n\bar X = n\bar X - n\bar X = \class{hl}{0} \]

    What we did: deviations from the mean always sum to zero (since \(\sum X_i = n\bar X\)). So the cross term vanishes.

  5. \[ V = \underbrace{\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}}_{\class{hl}{U}} + \underbrace{\Big(\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\Big)^2}_{\class{hl}{W}} \]

    What we did: renamed the two pieces. \(\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2 = (n-1)S^2\) by the definition of S². And \(n\frac{(\bar X-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{(\bar X - \mu)^2}{\sigma^2/n}\), the square of a standardized \(\bar X\).

  6. \[ W = Z^2 \text{ with } Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \sim N(0,1) \;\Rightarrow\; \class{hl}{W \sim \chi^2(1)} \]

    What we did: step 1 says the thing inside is standard Normal, and a squared standard Normal is χ²(1) (chapter 7).

  7. \[ M_V(t) = M_U(t)\cdot M_W(t) \;\Rightarrow\; \class{hl}{(1-2t)^{-n/2}} = M_U(t)\cdot\class{hl}{(1-2t)^{-1/2}} \]

    What we did: U depends only on S², W only on \(\bar X\), and step 2 made them independent. So the MGF of the sum is the product. Then we filled in the two MGFs we know.

  8. \[ M_U(t) = \frac{(1-2t)^{-n/2}}{(1-2t)^{-1/2}} = (1-2t)^{\class{hl}{-\frac{n-1}{2}}} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \class{hl}{\chi^2(n-1)} \]

    What we did: divided, subtracting exponents: \(-\frac n2 + \frac12 = -\frac{n-1}{2}\). That's the χ²(n − 1) MGF, so by uniqueness U is χ²(n − 1).

Step 4 · Assemble the t

Putting the pieces together

Goal: rearrange \(\frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt n}\) into the shape \(\frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/(n-1)}}\).

Plan: multiply the top and bottom by the same factor involving σ. It cancels overall, but it turns each part into something we recognize.

pink = what changed from the line before

  1. \[ \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} = \frac{(\bar X-\mu)\,\class{hl}{\sqrt n/\sigma}}{S\class{hl}{/\sigma}} \]

    What we did: multiplied top and bottom by \(\frac{\sqrt n}{\sigma}\). Multiplying a fraction's top and bottom by the same thing doesn't change it.

  2. \[ = \frac{\class{hl}{(\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}}{\class{hl}{\sqrt{S^2/\sigma^2}}} \]

    What we did: rewrote the top as a standardized \(\bar X\), and the bottom as a square root.

  3. \[ = \frac{(\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\class{hl}{(n-1)}S^2/\sigma^2}{\class{hl}{n-1}}}} \]

    What we did: multiplied and divided by n − 1 inside the root, so the χ² from step 3 appears.

  4. \[ = \frac{\class{hl}{Z}}{\sqrt{\class{hl}{U}/(n-1)}}, \quad Z \sim N(0,1),\ U\sim\chi^2(n-1),\ Z\perp\!\!\!\perp U \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim \class{hl}{t(n-1)} \]

    What we did: recognized the three ingredients from steps 1–3. This is exactly the definition of t from chapter 11, with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

Recap
  • Step 1: \(\bar X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\), by MGFs.
  • Step 2: \(\bar X \perp\!\!\!\perp S^2\): covariance 0, and jointly Normal, so independent.
  • Step 3: \(V = U + W\) with \(V \sim \chi^2(n)\), \(W \sim \chi^2(1)\), so \(U = (n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\).
  • Step 4: rearrange into \(Z/\sqrt{U/(n-1)}\), which is t(n − 1).

Summary and formula sheet

Every distribution here is built from standard Normals by squaring, adding or dividing, and every density's constant is a Gamma or Beta function that makes the area 1.

How to approach a derivation question

A checklist that covers every derivation here
  1. What's independent? Write the joint density as a product of the individual densities.
  2. One variable transformed (like \(X^2\))? Use the CDF method: CDF first, then differentiate.
  3. A ratio or combination of two variables (like t or F)? Use the five-step Jacobian recipe with a helper variable.
  4. A sum of independent variables? Multiply MGFs and recognize the result (uniqueness).
  5. An integral from 0 to ∞ with a power and an exponential? Use the scaled Gamma integral: \(\theta^a\Gamma(a)\).
  6. A ratio of Gammas like \(\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\)? Write it as \(B(a,b)\).
  7. Moments? Use \(E(X^k) = 2^k\Gamma(\frac n2+k)/\Gamma(\frac n2)\) for χ², and check the existence condition when k is negative.

Common mistakes

  • MGFs of independent sums multiply; they don't add (chapter 8).
  • \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\), not \(n!\).
  • Zero covariance means independent only for jointly Normal variables (chapter 13).
  • In a substitution, change dx and the limits. In a change of variables, don't forget |J|.
  • The Normal constant is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\), not \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\) (chapter 11).
  • In \(F(n_1, n_2)\) the order matters: numerator first.
